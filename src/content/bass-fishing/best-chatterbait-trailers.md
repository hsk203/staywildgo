A ChatterBait, also called a bladed jig or vibrating jig, works because of its blade: a hexagonal metal blade mounted on the jig head that vibrates, flashes, and kicks the jig from side to side on the retrieve. The rod and line matter too: a 7-foot medium-heavy setup from our [best baitcaster combo under $150](/bass-fishing/best-baitcaster-combo-under-150/) guide handles most bladed-jig fishing, and 12 to 15 lb fluorocarbon from our [best fluorocarbon line for bass fishing](/bass-fishing/best-fluorocarbon-line-for-bass-fishing/) guide is the standard line for the job. The trailer is what finishes the job. It adds the bulk and the profile that turn vibration into a meal, it changes how the jig falls and swims, and it lets you imitate whatever the bass are eating. The wrong trailer hurts more than no trailer at all — a trailer that is too big or too stiff overpowers the blade and kills the hunting action, and a trailer rigged crooked rolls the jig and blows out the retrieve. The right trailer disappears into the presentation, and the bass eat the whole package.

## Trailer Categories: Three Shapes That Cover Everything

Every ChatterBait trailer falls into one of three categories, and understanding them matters more than memorizing brand names.

**Paddletail swimbaits** have a boot-shaped tail that kicks on the retrieve. They add the most action and the most lift, and they are the best choice when you want the trailer to do visible work — imitating a swimming shad or bluegill in open water. The Zako, RaZor ShadZ, Skinny Dipper, and Keitech FAT all live in this family, with different tail shapes and body profiles.

**Craws** imitate crawfish: compact bodies with claws or flapping appendages that flutter as the jig moves. They add bulk without much swimming action, which keeps the blade's vibration as the star of the show. They are the right call in stained water, around rock and wood, and whenever bass are feeding on crawfish — which is most of the spring.

**Split-tail and fluke-style trailers** have a forked or split tail that wags subtly instead of kicking hard. They add profile with the least action, which makes them the choice when bass want a natural look rather than a loud one: clear water, pressured fish, and slow retrieves. The Zako's forked tail and the Spunk Shad's pintail both work this way, even though the baits look different at a glance.

Most anglers need one trailer from each category in the box. That is three packs, and it covers nearly every ChatterBait situation you will meet.

## How to Choose a Trailer by Forage

Match the hatch is not a slogan — it is the fastest way to pick a trailer. Look at what the bass are eating, then pick the trailer that looks like it.

**Shad.** When bass are chasing shad in open water, around bait balls, or over points, pick a shad-profile swimbait: the RaZor ShadZ, the Skinny Dipper, or the Keitech FAT. White, pearl, and electric shad colors match the forage, and a paddletail that kicks steadily reads as a baitfish swimming for its life. The RaZor ShadZ is the bulkiest of the three, so it suits bigger shad and dirtier water; the Skinny Dipper is the slimmest, so it suits clear water and smaller baitfish.

**Bluegill.** Around docks, grass beds, and shallow cover where bluegill live, pick a taller, deeper-bodied trailer: the Zako or the Goat. Both have the profile of a panfish — deep body, steady action — and green pumpkin, bluegill, and watermelon colors match the forage. The Goat's twin kicking tails add a bluegill-like flutter that works well when bass are feeding on panfish around cover.

**Crawfish.** In spring, around rock, riprap, and wood, bass feed heavily on crawfish. Pick the Speed Craw. Its compact body and flapping claws look like a crawfish fleeing across the bottom, and green pumpkin, Alabama craw, and black-and-blue colors match the local crawfish. The Speed Craw is also the right call in stained water, where its bulk and vibration help bass find it.

## How to Choose a Trailer by Water Clarity

Water clarity changes what the bass can see, and the trailer should change with it.

In **clear water**, bass get a long look at the bait, so subtlety wins. Pick trailers with natural colors — green pumpkin, watermelon, pearl — and modest action: the Skinny Dipper, the Keitech FAT, or the Zako in a natural color. A big, loud trailer in clear water looks wrong, and pressured bass will follow it without eating. Slow the retrieve down and let the blade do the attracting while the trailer does the convincing.

In **stained water**, bass find the bait by vibration and silhouette as much as by sight. Pick trailers with more bulk and more action: the Speed Craw, the Goat, or the RaZor ShadZ. Darker colors — black-and-blue, Junebug, green pumpkin with heavy flake — show up better against a stained background, and a trailer that moves more water helps bass track the jig. This is also where the ChatterBait itself shines brightest, since the blade's vibration calls fish in from further away than they could see.

In **muddy water**, go bold or go home: dark colors, maximum bulk, and a slow retrieve that keeps the jig in the strike zone. The Speed Craw in black-and-blue is the standard pick, and a larger trailer on a 1/2 oz jig keeps the presentation big enough for bass to find. If the water is truly muddy, a ChatterBait with a craw trailer is one of the few presentations that still produces, because the blade's vibration does the finding and the trailer's profile does the closing.

## How to Rig a Trailer Straight

A crooked trailer ruins a ChatterBait. The jig rolls, the blade stops hunting, and the retrieve blows out — the bait spins or planes to the surface instead of swimming. Rigging straight is a skill worth practicing.

Push the trailer onto the hook in one smooth motion, keeping the bait centered on the hook shank. The hook point should exit the top of the bait on the centerline, not off to one side. Then hold the jig up and look at it from behind: the trailer should hang level and centered behind the jig head, with the tail pointing straight back. If it leans left or right, pull it off and re-seat it. Some trailers have a molded slot or a flat side that shows you the centerline — use it.

For ElaZtech trailers like the RaZor ShadZ and the Goat, warming the bait in your hands for a few seconds makes it easier to thread straight, and the material's stretch lets you adjust it without tearing. For standard plastics like the Zako and the Speed Craw, a drop of super glue on the keeper holds the trailer in place through multiple fish. Check the trailer after every fish: a torn or slipped trailer fishes crooked, and a crooked trailer catches nothing.

## Matching Trailer Size to Jig Weight

A 3/8 oz ChatterBait is the standard all-around size, and a 3.5 to 4.5 inch trailer is the standard match for it. That combination fishes shallow to mid-depth water with a steady retrieve and covers most situations. When you step up to a 1/2 oz jig — for deeper water, faster retrieves, or wind — step the trailer up too: a 4.5-inch Zako, a 5.5-inch Spunk Shad, or a 4.25-inch Goat keeps the profile balanced with the heavier head. A small trailer on a heavy jig looks starved; a big trailer on a light jig overpowers the blade and kills the action.

For finesse bladed jigs like the Mini Max, downsize everything: a 3-inch Zako or a 3-inch Goat keeps the package in proportion. The rule is simple — the trailer should look like it belongs on the jig, not like it is towing it. When in doubt, fish the smaller trailer. Bass eat small meals confidently and ignore oversized ones.

## Retrieve: Let the Blade Work

The ChatterBait retrieve is simple: cast, let the jig fall to the depth you want, and reel it back steadily. The blade does the work — vibration, flash, and the hunting side-to-side kick. Your job is to keep the retrieve steady and the jig at the right depth. A slow roll keeps the jig deep; a faster retrieve brings it up in the water column. When the jig contacts grass, rip it free with a sharp snap of the rod — bass often strike the moment the jig breaks free.

Vary the retrieve when the steady retrieve stops producing. A stop-and-go retrieve, where you pause and let the jig fall for a second before resuming, triggers following fish. A yo-yo retrieve off the bottom works on deep structure. And a fast burn across a shallow flat triggers reaction strikes from bass that will not chase a slow bait. The trailer affects all of these: a paddletail adds lift and lets you fish slower, a craw adds bulk without lift, and a fluke-style trailer lets the jig fall faster on the pause. Pick the trailer for the retrieve you plan to fish, not just for the forage.
