If you could own only one soft plastic for bass, the Gary Yamamoto Senko would be the pick most anglers name. The answer to how to rig a Senko worm is simple: start weightless on a 3/0 to 4/0 EWG hook with 12 lb fluorocarbon, cast it out, and let it fall on slack line. That one setup catches bass in ponds, lakes, and rivers all year. But the Senko is far more than a one-rig bait. Rigged wacky, weighted Texas style, on a drop shot, or cut in half on a Ned rig, the same 5-inch stick bait covers shallow cover, deep structure, and pressured fish that refuse everything else. This guide covers all five setups — the hook and weight for each, the retrieve, and the mistakes that cost bites.

The Senko works because of what it is, not because of what you do with it. Gary Yamamoto introduced the bait in 1996, and Bassmaster notes it remains one of the best-selling soft plastics of all time. It comes in five sizes — 3, 4, 5, 6, and 7 inches — with more than 120 colors listed on Yamamoto's site, and the 5-inch model is the workhorse most anglers reach for first. A 10-pack of 5-inch Senkos runs $7–$9 at major retailers, as of November 2026. The plastic is heavily salt-impregnated, which makes the bait dense: a 5-inch Senko weighs close to 3/8 oz (per published spec tables), so it casts easily even with no weight at all. On slack line it falls horizontally, shimmying side to side as it sinks — a dying-baitfish wobble that bass find hard to ignore. Field & Stream calls the salt content and the profile the two key elements behind that fall. Your job is mostly to stay out of the way: rig it straight, cast it out, and let it sink.

## Why the Senko Works

Before the rigs, it helps to understand what makes this plain-looking stick bait so effective. The Senko has no tail, no legs, and no built-in swimming action. Its action is the fall. Because the salt is distributed evenly through the body, the bait sinks level instead of nose-first, and the soft plastic quivers as it descends. That is why the retrieve matters more than the rig: almost every Senko setup is fished the same way — cast, let it fall on a slack line, watch the line, lift, and repeat.

Line choice supports that presentation. Fluorocarbon sinks, which keeps the bait falling naturally instead of dragging it toward the surface the way floating monofilament can. It is also nearly invisible underwater and stretches less than mono, so you feel the faint tick of a bass picking up the bait on the fall. Our guide to the [best fluorocarbon line for bass fishing](/bass-fishing/best-fluorocarbon-line-for-bass-fishing/) compares the main options by use, but the short version is that 12 lb fluorocarbon is the default Senko line, with lighter line for finesse rigs and heavier line in thick cover. If you are still deciding between casting and spinning gear for these rigs, the comparison in [baitcaster vs spinning reel](/bass-fishing/baitcaster-vs-spinning-reel/) lays out which tool fits each setup.

Color is simpler than tackle shops make it look. Green pumpkin is the default in most water, watermelon covers clear water, and black-and-blue or Junebug earns its place in stained water and low light. The full rundown of lure choices by situation is in our [best bass lures](/bass-fishing/best-bass-lures/) guide. For Senkos specifically, start with green pumpkin in a 5-inch size and add one dark color for stained water. That covers most days.

## What You Need: Hooks, Weights, and Line

A Senko setup is cheap to assemble, and most of the tackle overlaps between rigs. A pack of 5-inch Senkos, a pack of 3/0 EWG hooks, a pack of 1/0 wacky hooks, a few bullet weights, and a spool of 12 lb fluorocarbon will cover four of the five rigs in this guide. Add a pack of small O-rings and a wacky rigging tool and you are set for the fifth detail that saves baits.

Hook size follows bait size. For a 5-inch Senko Texas-rigged, most guides recommend a 3/0 to 4/0 EWG hook, and that range shows up consistently across rigging guides: a 3/0 EWG pairs with a 5-inch Senko, while a 4/0 gives a slightly wider gap for thicker cover and bigger hooks sets. Go smaller for a 4-inch Senko and larger for a 6-inch model. For wacky rigging, the hook shrinks: a 1/0 or 2/0 finesse wide-gap hook is the standard, with 1/0 the most versatile single size. The reason is weight — a heavy hook drags the middle of the bait down and ruins the level fall that makes the wacky rig work.

Bullet weights for a weighted Texas rig usually run from 1/16 oz to 3/8 oz, with 1/8 to 1/4 oz covering most Senko fishing. Use the lightest weight that gets the job done: 1/8 oz keeps a slow fall in shallow water, 3/16 to 1/4 oz is the all-around range for water in the 5 to 15 foot zone and light grass, and 3/8 oz is reserved for punching through thick vegetation. Tungsten weights are smaller than lead of the same weight and transmit bottom contact better, but lead works fine on a budget — the fish do not read the label.

For line, the working ranges are straightforward. Texas rigs take 8 to 12 lb fluorocarbon in open water and up to 15 lb when you are fishing around wood and grass. Some anglers run 16 to 17 lb fluorocarbon in heavy cover, but that is the exception rather than the rule. Wacky and drop-shot presentations on spinning gear usually run 6 to 10 lb fluorocarbon. A medium-heavy baitcasting rod handles the Texas rigs, while a medium-power spinning rod is the better tool for wacky and drop-shot Senkos, especially in the 6-foot-8 to 7-foot range that most wacky guides recommend.

## 1. Weightless Texas Rig: The Default Senko Setup

The weightless Texas rig is the first Senko setup to learn and the one you will return to most often. It is weedless, it casts well because the bait itself weighs close to 3/8 oz, and it fishes the full water column on the fall. Use it around docks, overhanging trees, weed edges, and shallow flats — anywhere bass hold in less than about 8 feet of water.

### How to rig it

1. Tie a 3/0 or 4/0 EWG hook to your line with a Palomar knot and wet the knot before cinching it down.
2. Hold the Senko with the thick, flat end forward — that is the nose.
3. Push the hook point into the center of the nose and thread it out through the bottom of the bait about a quarter inch back.
4. Slide the bait up the hook shank and rotate the hook so the point faces the bait, then push the point back into the body and skin-hook it just under the surface so the point is buried but ready to pop free.
5. Lay the bait on a flat surface and check it from the side and from above. It should hang perfectly straight. A crooked Senko spins on the fall and bass ignore it.

### How to fish it

Cast past the target and let the bait fall on slack line — do not reel while it sinks. Watch the line where it enters the water: a bite usually shows as the line jumping sideways, twitching, or suddenly going slack. Count the fall down in your head so you know how deep the bait is, and once it reaches bottom, lift the rod tip slowly to raise the bait a foot or two, then let it fall again on slack. Two or three lifts per cast are usually enough. If nothing bites, reel in and cast somewhere new. The Senko is a slow bait, but covering water matters: short casts to visible cover beat long blind casts.

This is also the rig to pair with the wider Texas rig system. If you want the full step-by-step with photos of the weedless rigging, our [texas rig in a minute](/bass-fishing/texas-rig-in-a-minute/) guide covers the same mechanics in a quick-reference format.

## 2. Wacky Rig: The Finesse Presentation

The wacky rig is the Senko at its simplest: a hook through the middle of the bait, nothing else. It produces the most exaggerated action of any Senko rig because both ends of the bait flutter as it falls. It shines on pressured fish, around docks, and in clear water where bass have seen every moving bait in the box. It is also the best Senko rig for beginners because there is almost nothing to get wrong — except rigging it crooked or fishing it too fast.

### How to rig it

1. Slide a small rubber O-ring to the exact middle of the Senko. A wacky rigging tool makes this a two-second job, but you can roll the ring on by hand.
2. Slide a 1/0 or 2/0 wacky hook under the O-ring so the hook sits crosswise to the bait. Do not push the hook through the plastic itself.
3. Check that the bait hangs level when you hold it up by the line. If one end droops, slide the O-ring until the bait balances.
4. If you fish around wood or docks, use a weedless wacky hook with a light wire guard. In open water, an exposed hook hooks more fish.

The O-ring is worth the small cost. Hooking straight through the middle of a Senko tears the bait after one or two fish; with an O-ring the hook never punctures the plastic, and anglers routinely catch three or four bass on a single Senko. Some anglers use two O-rings crossed in an X for an even more secure hold. Either way, keep a pack of O-rings in the box — they cost little and they double the life of your Senkos.

### How to fish it

Fish the wacky rig on spinning tackle with 6 to 10 lb fluorocarbon and a medium-power rod. Cast it out, let it fall on slack line, and watch it. The fall is the bite window. Once it hits bottom, twitch the rod tip once or twice so both ends of the bait quiver, then let it settle and fall again. In deeper water or wind, a weighted wacky jighead in 1/16 to 1/8 oz gets the bait down while keeping the same fluttering fall. Set the hook with a firm sweep rather than a hard snap — the light hook and light line punish an aggressive hookset with pulled hooks.

## 3. Weighted Texas Rig: For Grass, Brush, and Depth

When the weightless rig cannot reach the fish — thick grass, brush piles, or water deeper than about 10 feet — the weighted Texas rig takes over. A bullet weight ahead of the hook pulls the Senko down through cover and keeps it in the strike zone longer. The tradeoff is a faster fall and less shimmy, so use the lightest weight that reaches the depth you need.

### How to rig it

1. Thread a bullet weight onto your line, pointed end first, then tie on a bobber stopper or a small bead if you want to peg the weight in place.
2. Tie on a 3/0 or 4/0 EWG hook.
3. Rig the Senko weedless exactly as in the weightless setup, checking that the bait sits straight on the hook.
4. For punching through matted vegetation, peg the weight tight against the nose of the bait with a bobber stopper and step up to a 3/8 oz tungsten bullet. For ordinary grass edges and brush, leave the weight unpegged so it slides — the clicking of weight against bead and the free fall of the bait both help.

### How to fish it

Pitch or flip the rig into pockets in the grass rather than casting across the top. Let it fall on a semi-slack line so you can still feel the weight tick through the stems, then lift and drop it through the cover. In brush piles, let the bait fall to the bottom and drag it slowly out, pausing whenever the weight contacts a limb. Most bites on a weighted Texas rig feel like a subtle tap or like the line simply getting heavy — reel down until you feel the fish, then set the hook hard enough to drive the point through the plastic and the jaw. Heavy cover is where 15 lb fluorocarbon earns its place, and a medium-heavy baitcasting rod with a fast tip is the right tool for the job.

## 4. Drop Shot: The Deep-Water Senko

The drop shot turns the Senko into a deep-water finesse bait. The weight sits on the bottom while the bait hovers above it, quivering in place — a presentation for suspended bass, deep points, and fish holding on offshore structure that a Texas rig falls past too quickly. Use a 4-inch Senko here rather than the 5-inch model; the smaller bait matches the finesse presentation and stays in the strike zone longer.

### How to rig it

1. Tie a size 1 drop-shot hook to 6 to 8 lb fluorocarbon with a Palomar knot, leaving a long tag end — 12 to 18 inches is a good starting point for the leader below the hook.
2. Thread the tag end back down through the hook eye so the hook stands out horizontally from the line.
3. Nose-hook the 4-inch Senko through the thick end, just enough to hold it. The bait should hang horizontally.
4. Tie or clip a small cylindrical drop-shot weight to the end of the tag line. Match the weight to the depth and wind — lighter for shallow, calm water, heavier when it is deep or windy.

### How to fish it

Lower the rig straight down or cast it out and let the weight settle on the bottom. Then barely move it: tiny shakes of the rod tip make the Senko quiver without moving the weight. Hold the bait in place for 10 to 20 seconds at a time — the drop shot is a patience rig, and the bites often come when you think nothing is happening. Watch the line for the slightest tick or slack, and set the hook with a short upward sweep. Because the line is light, keep the drag set loose enough to protect 6 lb fluorocarbon when a big fish surges.

## 5. Ned Rig (Half Senko): The Finesse Closer

The Ned rig is the simplest finesse setup in bass fishing: half a Senko on a mushroom-head jig. The original Ned rig was exactly that — half of a Yamamoto Senko threaded onto a light mushroom head — and the DIY version still works as well as any store-bought Ned plastic. It is the rig to reach for on tough days, cold fronts, and heavily pressured water, when bass will not chase anything with a big profile.

### How to rig it

1. Cut a 5-inch Senko in half with scissors or bite it cleanly at the midpoint. You get two Ned baits from one Senko.
2. Thread the cut end onto a 1/16 to 1/8 oz mushroom-head jig, pushing the bait up to the head so it sits straight.
3. Check the rig on a flat surface: the flat mushroom head should sit flush on the bottom with the bait standing nearly upright. If the bait leans, re-seat it until it stands straight.
4. Use a 1/16 oz head in shallow, calm water and step up to 1/8 oz for wind or deeper water. The mushroom-head range runs from 1/16 to 1/4 oz, but the light end is where the Ned rig does its best work.

### How to fish it

Fish it on spinning tackle with 6 to 8 lb fluorocarbon. Cast it out, let it fall to the bottom, and then do almost nothing: a slow drag, a short hop, and long pauses. The bait standing upright on the bottom is the whole presentation — it looks like a small baitfish or crawfish feeding, and bass pick it up without the chase. Keep the retrieve painfully slow. If you think you are fishing it too slowly, slow down more. Set the hook with a steady sweep when the line tightens or the rod tip loads up on its own.

## The Retrieve: Slack Line Is Everything

Every rig in this guide shares one rule: the bite usually comes on the fall, and the fall only works on slack line. Reeling while the Senko sinks pulls it nose-down and kills the shimmy. Cast, then feed out line or drop the rod tip so the bait falls freely. Watch the line, not the bait — you will rarely see the bite. The line jumping, twitching, or going slack for no reason is a bass. Reel down until you feel weight, then set the hook.

The second rule is to fish slowly but cover water. A Senko cast that soaks for a minute in dead water catches nothing. Give each cast two or three lifts, then move on. Docks, weed edges, laydowns, and points are all worth a cast, and [spring bass after the spawn](/bass-fishing/spring-bass-after-the-spawn/) covers where bass hold when the season turns and the Senko bite gets going in earnest.

## Common Mistakes That Cost Bites

**Rigging the bait crooked.** A Senko that curves or twists on the hook spins as it falls instead of shimmying. Check every bait from the side before the first cast. If it is not straight, re-rig it.

**Reeling during the fall.** The fall is the presentation. If you reel while the bait sinks, you drag it through the water nose-first and the bite window closes. Let it fall on slack line every time.

**Setting the hook too early.** Bass often pick up a Senko and swim with it before turning it in their mouth. If you set on the first twitch of the line, you pull the bait out of the fish's mouth. Reel down until you feel solid weight, then set.

**Using too heavy a weight.** A heavy bullet weight turns the Senko into a fast-sinking jig and removes the slow shimmy that triggers bites. Start light and add weight only when the bait cannot reach the fish.

**Fishing it too fast.** The Senko is a slow bait. If you are working it like a crankbait, you are fishing it wrong. Short lifts, long pauses, and patience catch the fish that fast retrieves miss.

**Skipping the O-ring on a wacky rig.** Hooking through the middle of the bait tears it after a fish or two. An O-ring costs almost nothing and keeps one Senko fishing for three or four bass.

## Choosing Colors and Sizes

Size first, color second. The 5-inch Senko is the default for largemouth in most situations. Drop to the 4-inch model for smallmouth, pressured fish, and drop-shot or Ned presentations. Step up to the 6-inch model when you want a bigger profile for big fish or stained water. The 3-inch and 7-inch sizes have their places — the 3-inch for finesse and panfish-heavy water, the 7-inch for trophy hunting — but most anglers can fish an entire season on 4- and 5-inch Senkos.

For color, keep it simple. Green pumpkin is the all-around default, watermelon handles clear water, and black-and-blue or Junebug covers stained water and low light. In muddy water, brighter colors and the [best spinnerbait color for muddy water in spring](/bass-fishing/best-spinnerbait-color-for-muddy-water-in-spring/) approach — matching visibility to the water — applies to soft plastics too. Match the hatch when you can see what the bass are eating, and when in doubt, fish green pumpkin.
