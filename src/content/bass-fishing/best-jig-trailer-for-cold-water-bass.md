Cold water changes the jig game completely. When the water drops under 60°F, bass slow down, eat less, and stop chasing anything that moves too fast — and the trailer on the back of your jig is what decides whether a sluggish bass commits or just stares at it. If you could carry only one cold-water trailer, make it the V&M Cherry Bug: BassResource's Glenn May reaches for it whenever bass turn inactive after a cold front or in winter, because its low-action body falls naturally and matches the mood of the fish. The other five trailers in this guide cover the rest of the cold-water situations: deep football-jig water, finesse presentations, flipping cover, and stained water where scent matters. ## The Cold-Water Rule: Less Action, Slower Fall

The rule is simple, and it comes straight from Glenn May's soft-plastic guide on BassResource: match the trailer's action to the fish's activity level. When bass are actively feeding, a high-action trailer like the Strike King Rage Craw works well, because its flapping claws match an aggressive fish. When bass are not feeding actively — after a big cold front comes through, or in the middle of winter — May switches to something with far less action, like a V&M Cherry Bug or a V&M Mud Bug, because they fall naturally in the water and match the activity level of the fish. That one decision, active versus inactive, is the whole foundation of cold-water trailer selection.

The biology behind it is straightforward. A bass is cold-blooded, so its metabolism tracks the water temperature. Below about 60°F, bass move less, feed less often, and strike with less commitment. Below 50°F, they turn genuinely lethargic: they hold tight to cover or structure, often suspend just off the bottom, and a lure has to sit in front of them for a long time before they bother to eat it. A trailer with big flapping appendages looks like a lot of work to chase, and in cold water a bass will not spend that energy. A compact chunk or craw that drifts down slowly, wiggles almost nothing, and looks like an easy meal is the right answer. Check the official site of any lure maker for the exact water-temperature guidance on their products, since temperature effects vary by region and species.

That is also why trailer size shrinks in cold water. In summer, a big creature bait on a flipping jig makes sense because bass are eating large meals. In winter, the forage is smaller and the bass want a smaller package. The trailers in this guide run from about 2.5 inches to 4.2 inches, and none of them is a big, sprawling creature bait. A smaller trailer also keeps the jig's total profile compact, which matters when bass are inspecting the lure from inches away instead of charging it from feet away. For the seasonal picture of where bass go as the water cools and warms again, our [spring bass fishing tips](/bass-fishing/spring-bass-fishing-tips/) cover the transition periods when cold-water rules start to loosen.

One more thing the cold-water rule changes: color discipline. In cold, clear water, bass get a long look at your jig, so the trailer should match the jig skirt and look like one natural piece. May's advice is to match the trailer color to the jig itself — green pumpkin on a green pumpkin jig, black on a black-and-blue jig — rather than mixing bright colors that break up the profile. In stained cold water the rules bend a little, and the color section later in this guide covers that. If the water you fish turns muddy in spring, our guide to the [best bass lures for murky water](/bass-fishing/best-bass-lures-for-murky-water/) covers the stained-water side of the same problem.

## Line and Rod Pairing: Fluorocarbon, 12 to 17 lb

Cold-water jig fishing is a feel game. The bites are light — often just a slight heaviness on the line, or the line going slack as a bass picks the jig up and swims toward you. Fluorocarbon is the standard choice because it sinks, which keeps the jig in contact with the bottom, and because it is nearly invisible underwater, which matters when bass are studying the lure in clear cold water. It also stretches less than monofilament, so a soft pickup transmits to the rod tip instead of disappearing into line stretch. Our guide to the [best fluorocarbon line for bass fishing](/bass-fishing/best-fluorocarbon-line-for-bass-fishing/) compares the leading lines by diameter, abrasion resistance, and price.

The test range for cold-water jig fishing is 12 to 17 lb. Use 12 lb fluorocarbon for finesse jigs and clear water, where a thinner line gives the jig a more natural fall and the bass a less visible connection to your rod. Use 15 to 17 lb for football jigs fished deep and for flipping jigs around wood and rock, where abrasion resistance matters more than invisibility. Braid has its place for flipping heavy cover in warm water, but in cold water the lack of stretch makes it easier to pull the jig away from a bass that has only half-committed to the bite, so fluorocarbon is the safer default for most cold-water jig work.

The rod matters just as much. A 7-foot medium-heavy rod with a fast tip is the all-purpose cold-water jig rod: long enough to make long casts to deep structure, stiff enough to drive a jig hook home, and sensitive enough to feel a bass pick up the bait on a slack line. Pair it with a 7:1 gear-ratio baitcasting reel, which picks up line fast enough to catch up to a bass swimming toward the boat — the most common way cold-water bites are missed. Keep the drag set firm but not locked, because a cold-water bass hooked on light line in deep water needs steady pressure, not a yank.

## The 6 Best Jig Trailers for Cold Water Bass

### 1. V&M Cherry Bug

The Cherry Bug is the cold-water trailer, and it earns the top spot on authority, not price. In his BassResource soft-plastic guide, Glenn May names the V&M Cherry Bug — along with its sibling the Mud Bug — as the trailer he uses when bass are inactive, specifically after a cold front or in wintertime. The reason is its lack of action: the bait has a compact, chunky body with small appendages that barely move, so it falls naturally and looks like the kind of easy meal a lethargic bass will actually eat. It is the trailer that defines the cold-water rule, and it is the one to buy first if you own only one.

**Specs:** Tackle Warehouse lists the V&M Cherry Bug jig trailer at $5–$6. V&M is a small bait company that has been making soft plastics since 1990, and TackleTour's review of the V&M Mud Bug — the Cherry Bug's close relative — lists that bait at 4 inches in the standard size and 3 inches in the baby size, with 13 colors. The Cherry Bug comes in green pumpkin and other natural craw colors; match it to the jig skirt.

**When to throw it:** Whenever the water is under 55°F, after a cold front, or when bass are showing on the graph but not chasing. It pairs with every jig type in this guide — football, finesse, and flipping — because its profile is neutral and its action is minimal. Fish it on a slow drag along the bottom with long pauses, and let the jig do the work. The trailer's job is to add bulk and a crawfish silhouette, not movement.

**Watch out for:** The Cherry Bug is a specialist, and in warm water it can look too dead. When the water warms past 60°F and bass start feeding actively, switch to a trailer with more action, such as the Rage Tail Chunk later in this list. V&M baits are also sold through fewer retailers than the big brands, so check availability before a trip — if your local shop does not carry it, Tackle Warehouse lists it online.

### 2. Zoom Super Chunk

The Super Chunk is the budget workhorse of this list, and it is the cheapest way to slow a jig's fall in cold water. It is a chunk-style trailer with thin wings that flutter gently as the jig sinks, adding bulk without adding the wild flapping action that turns off cold bass. Dick's Sporting Goods lists the Super Chunk at 3-1/2 inches long, 5 per pack, and describes its thin wings as providing fluttering action that bass cannot resist. The wings also create drag in the water, which slows the jig's fall — exactly what you want when bass need extra time to commit.

**Specs:** Tackle Warehouse lists the Zoom Super Chunk at $3–$4, and MidwayUSA lists the smaller Super Chunk Jr. at $3–$4 as well. The standard Super Chunk is 3-1/2 inches and comes 5 per pack; the Jr. version is 2-3/4 inches for finesse jigs and smaller profiles. Zoom has made soft plastics since 1977, and the Super Chunk comes in 13 colors at Tackle Warehouse, including green pumpkin and black blue flake.

**When to throw it:** On a football jig dragged across deep points and ledges, where the bulk slows the fall and keeps the jig in the strike zone longer. It is also a good trailer for a swim jig fished slowly along a weed edge in late fall, when the water is cooling but the bass are still on the edges. Because it costs only $3–$4 a pack, it is the trailer to use when you are fishing snaggy rock and expect to lose a few jigs — the trailer is the cheap part of the rig.

**Watch out for:** The Super Chunk's wings are thin, and a bass can bite them off after a fish or two. Carry a spare pack. Also, the bait is salt-impregnated, which helps bass hold on, but it also means the baits can dry out and harden if the bag is left open — keep the bag sealed between trips.

### 3. Gary Yamamoto Fat Baby Craw

The Fat Baby Craw is the compact craw option, and it is built for the way cold-water bass actually eat: a small, fat, easy meal that fits in a small mouth without a fight. Yamamoto describes it as a fatter-bodied craw that makes a great compact jig trailer and breaks through dense grass to reach the bass underneath. The fat body displaces water and gives the jig a bigger profile without adding length, which is the right trade in cold water — bulk without sprawl. Its small claws move subtly on the fall and almost not at all on the bottom, which keeps it inside the cold-water rule.

**Specs:** Tackle Warehouse lists the Yamamoto Fat Baby Craw at $5–$6 for a 7-pack. The bait is 3-3/4 inches long, and it comes in 7 colors including black with large blue flake, watermelon with black and red flake, and green pumpkin with black flake. Yamamoto's soft plastics are salt-heavy, which adds weight and helps the bait sink naturally on a jig.

**When to throw it:** On a flipping jig around docks, laydowns, and brush piles, where the compact body slips through cover and the fat profile looks like a crawfish backed into a corner. It also works on a finesse jig fished on rock and gravel in clear cold water, where its small size matches the small crawfish that live in that habitat. Green pumpkin is the default color; black with large blue flake suits stained water and low light.

**Watch out for:** The Fat Baby Craw's soft plastic is durable, but the claws are thin and can tear after a hard bite. Check the trailer after every fish and replace it when a claw goes missing — a one-clawed craw looks wrong to a bass that is already suspicious. Also, the bait is sold in a 7-pack, so it costs more per bait than the Zoom Super Chunk; save it for the cover where its compact profile matters most.

### 4. Strike King Rage Tail Chunk

The Rage Tail Chunk is the most active trailer on this list, and that is exactly why it sits at number four rather than number one. Its short, fat body and extra-large pincers swirl and twirl for eye-catching action, according to Strike King's own description, and that action is a liability in the coldest water but an asset at the warmer end of cold water — in the mid to upper 50s, when bass are starting to move but are not yet in full warm-water feeding mode. Think of it as the bridge trailer: the one you reach for when the water is warming and the Cherry Bug starts to look too quiet.

**Specs:** Tackle Warehouse and Dick's Sporting Goods list the Strike King Rage Tail Chunk at $7–$8 for a 7-pack. Dick's lists the bait at 2-1/2 inches long, while other retailers list it at 3 inches, so check the official site for the exact length of the color you buy. It comes in colors including green pumpkin and Falcon Lake Craw, and the Rage Tail line is Strike King's best-known soft-plastic family.

**When to throw it:** In water from about 55°F to 60°F, on a casting jig or a swim jig fished with a slow, steady retrieve. The pincers move even at slow speeds, which gives a lethargic bass something to key on without demanding a chase. It also works in stained cold water, where the extra movement helps bass find the jig. Pair it with a 3/8 oz jig in the mid-depth range, and fish it with short hops rather than long drags so the pincers keep working.

**Watch out for:** Do not throw this trailer in water under 50°F. The pincers that make it effective in the upper 50s look frantic to a bass that is barely moving, and you will get fewer bites than with the Cherry Bug or the Super Chunk. The Rage Craw — the Chunk's bigger, wilder cousin — is even worse in cold water, and belongs strictly in warm-water fishing. If the water is cold and the fish are not moving, put the Rage Tail Chunk back in the box and pick the Cherry Bug.

### 5. Berkley PowerBait MaxScent Meaty Chunk

The Meaty Chunk is the scent trailer, and scent is the cold-water angler's secret weapon. When bass are lethargic, they do not chase, but they do follow a scent trail, and Berkley's MaxScent material releases what the company calls a super-charged scent field that draws fish in from a distance. BassResource's review of the MaxScent line pairs a green pumpkin Meaty Chunk with a brush jig and notes that the bait's extra bumps add scent-releasing surface area for tentative fish — exactly the fish you meet in cold water. The bait's soft, durable material also gives it a lifelike feel, so a bass that picks it up holds on longer, which matters when the bite is so soft you barely feel it.

**Specs:** Sportsman's Warehouse lists the Berkley PowerBait MaxScent Meaty Chunk at $9–$10. The bait is 3 inches long and comes 6 per pack, in colors including green pumpkin, black blue fleck, sapphire blue, and watermelon red. It is the most expensive trailer on this list, and the price reflects the MaxScent formula rather than the size.

**When to throw it:** In stained or muddy cold water, where bass cannot see the jig well and have to find it by scent and vibration. It is also the right choice on heavily pressured lakes in winter, where the local bass have seen every jig in the shop and a scent trail is the one thing that still pulls them in. Fish it on a football jig in deep water, with a dead-slow drag and pauses of five to ten seconds — the scent needs time to spread, and the bass needs time to follow it.

**Watch out for:** MaxScent baits dry out faster than standard soft plastics, so keep them in the sealed bag and do not leave them on a jig in a hot car. The material is also softer than standard plastic, so a trailer may only last one or two fish. At $9–$10 a pack, that adds up — save the Meaty Chunk for stained water and pressured fish, and use the Super Chunk for everyday cold-water fishing.

### 6. Reaction Innovations Sweet Beaver 4.20

The Sweet Beaver is the flipping trailer, and it is the biggest profile on this list for a reason: it is built to be fished slowly through heavy cover, and heavy cover is where the biggest cold-water bass spend the winter. The bait's compact, flat body with forward-facing ribs creates pressure waves that move a lot of water, according to FishUSA's description, and its wide beaver-style tail flutters gently on the fall without the wild flapping of a high-action craw. Wired2Fish's review of the Sweet Beaver notes its innovative ribbed body and calls it one of the most imitated baits in bass fishing, and Reaction Innovations is credited as the inventor of the original beaver bait.

**Specs:** Tackle Warehouse lists the Reaction Innovations Sweet Beaver 4.20 at $5–$6. The bait is 4.2 inches long and comes 8 per pack in current retail listings, in 54 colors at Tackle Warehouse. Older reviews list a 10-pack, so check the pack count on the listing before you order. Green Pumpkin Red, Hematoma, and California 420 are among the most popular colors.

**When to throw it:** On a flipping jig pitched into brush piles, dock posts, and flooded timber in cold water, where the beaver's flat body slides through cover and its slow fall gives a bass holding tight to wood plenty of time to eat. It is also a strong choice on a football jig in deep water, where the wide tail acts like a parachute and slows the fall. Fish it with a dead-stick pause after it hits the bottom — in cold water, the bite often comes after the bait has sat still for ten seconds or more.

**Watch out for:** The Sweet Beaver is the bulkiest trailer here, so it can overpower a small finesse jig. Match it to a 1/2 oz or heavier jig, and use a 4/0 or 5/0 hook so the wide body does not block the hook point. The bait has no added scent, so in stained water it relies on its profile and water displacement alone — if the water is muddy, the Meaty Chunk's scent gives it an edge.

## Matching the Trailer to the Jig Type

The trailer does not work alone; it has to match the jig head behind it. Three jig types cover almost all cold-water fishing, and each one wants a different trailer.

The football jig is the deep-water tool. Its wide, football-shaped head drags across rock, gravel, and shell without snagging, and it is the jig for winter points, ledges, and channel swings in 15 to 30 feet of water. The best cold-water trailers for a football jig are the ones that add bulk and slow the fall: the Zoom Super Chunk, the Berkley MaxScent Meaty Chunk, and the Sweet Beaver. A 1/2 oz to 3/4 oz football jig with one of those trailers stays in contact with the bottom on a slow drag, and the trailer's bulk keeps the whole package falling slowly enough for a lethargic bass to intercept it. Fish it with a drag-and-pause retrieve, and watch the line on the pause — most winter football-jig bites come when the bait is sitting still.

The finesse jig is the clear-water tool. It is smaller and lighter, usually 1/4 oz to 5/16 oz, with a finer skirt and a smaller hook, and it is built for bass that have a long look at the lure. The best cold-water trailers for a finesse jig are the smallest ones on this list: the Zoom Super Chunk Jr. at 2-3/4 inches, the Yamamoto Fat Baby Craw, and the V&M Cherry Bug. The trailer should not overwhelm the jig — if the trailer is bigger than the skirt, the profile looks wrong. Fish a finesse jig on 12 lb fluorocarbon with a light drag, and fish it where the water is clearest: rock banks, gravel points, and the edges of deep docks.

The flipping jig is the cover tool. It is built to be pitched into brush, docks, and timber, and it needs a trailer that slides through cover without hanging up. The Sweet Beaver is the classic flipping-jig trailer, and the Fat Baby Craw is the compact alternative when the cover is tight and the bass are small. Use a 3/8 oz to 1/2 oz flipping jig with a trailer that matches the jig's bulk, and fish it with a vertical presentation: pitch it in, let it fall on a semi-slack line, and shake it once or twice before lifting it out. In cold water, a bass holding in a brush pile will not chase a jig that falls past it — the trailer has to fall slowly enough to stay in front of that fish for several seconds.

## Fall Rate Control: Bulk Slows the Fall

In cold water, the fall is the presentation. A bass that will not chase a fast-moving lure will still eat a jig that drifts down slowly past its nose, and the trailer is the main tool for controlling how fast the jig falls. The rule is simple: more bulk means a slower fall, because a bigger trailer creates more water resistance. That is why a chunk-style trailer is so valuable in winter — it acts like a parachute, and a 1/2 oz jig with a bulky trailer can fall as slowly as a 3/8 oz jig with a slim one.

Use that to your advantage. If the bass are suspended just off the bottom, or if they are following the jig down without committing, switch to a bulkier trailer — the Super Chunk, the Meaty Chunk, or the Sweet Beaver — and the slower fall gives them more time to decide. If the bass are tight to the bottom and you need the jig to get down fast through a school of baitfish, switch to a slimmer trailer like the Fat Baby Craw, or trim the trailer's body with scissors to reduce bulk. Some anglers pinch off the tips of a chunk trailer's wings to fine-tune the fall rate; a small trim makes a noticeable difference in how slowly the jig sinks.

Water temperature changes the fall rate too. Cold water is denser than warm water, so a jig falls slightly slower in winter than in summer — but not enough to matter on its own. What matters is that a cold bass needs the jig in front of it for longer, so the slower fall from a bulky trailer is worth more in winter than in any other season. Fish the fall, not the retrieve: most cold-water jig bites happen while the bait is sinking, so keep a semi-slack line, watch for the line to jump or go slack, and set the hook on anything that feels different.

## Color by Water Clarity

Cold water is usually clear water, and clear water means the bass can see your jig from a long way off — which is both an advantage and a problem. The advantage is that the bass will find the bait. The problem is that the bass will also see every flaw in it, including a trailer color that does not match the jig or the forage. The safe rule in clear cold water is to match the hatch and match the jig: green pumpkin in water with green-tinted crawfish, watermelon red where the crawfish run red, and black and blue in low light or under cloud cover. Glenn May's advice to match the trailer color to the jig skirt applies double in clear water, where a mismatched trailer stands out like a flag.

In stained cold water, the rules relax. Bass cannot see fine detail, so they key on silhouette and vibration, and a trailer with a little more contrast helps. Black and blue is the classic stained-water combination because it creates a strong silhouette against a murky background. Chartreuse-tipped claws add visibility without changing the bait's overall profile, and a trailer with a little flash — such as the Sapphire Blue Meaty Chunk — can help a bass find the jig in water where visibility is under two feet. The MaxScent Meaty Chunk earns its place in stained water twice over: its scent trail works when visibility does not.

In muddy cold water, after a winter rain, keep the jig simple. A black-and-blue jig with a black trailer is the most reliable combination, because it gives the bass the biggest, clearest target. Fish it slowly, with long pauses, and rely on the trailer's bulk and the jig's skirt to displace water — the bass will feel the bait before it sees it. If the water is muddy and cold at the same time, fish the shallowest muddy water you can find, because muddy water warms faster in winter sun than clear water does, and bass will move into it to warm up.

## What Not to Throw in Cold Water

The fastest way to waste a cold-water trip is to fish a warm-water trailer. The Strike King Rage Craw is the classic example: its big flapping claws are built to trigger aggressive bass in warm water, and in cold water they look like a baitfish having a panic attack. Glenn May's rule is explicit — Rage Craw when the fish are active, Cherry Bug or Mud Bug when they are not — and ignoring that rule is the most common jig-trailer mistake in winter. The same goes for swimbaits with hard-kicking paddle tails and creature baits with long, wild appendages: they are built for fish that chase, and cold-water bass do not chase.

The second mistake is fishing too fast. A jig trailer cannot fix a retrieve that is too quick. In water under 50°F, a good rule is to fish the jig half as fast as you think you should, then slow down again. Drag it, pause it, shake it once, and pause it again. If you are not bored, you are probably fishing too fast. The trailer's job is to look alive while the jig sits still — and the trailers on this list are all built to do exactly that.

The third mistake is using too heavy a jig. A 3/4 oz football jig has its place in deep winter water, but a heavy jig falls fast, and a fast fall is the opposite of what a lethargic bass wants. Start with a 3/8 oz jig in water under 15 feet and a 1/2 oz jig in deeper water, and let the trailer's bulk control the fall rate instead of adding lead. If the jig is falling too fast, change the trailer before you change the jig weight.

## Putting It Together

Start with the water temperature. If it is under 50°F, rig a V&M Cherry Bug or a Zoom Super Chunk on a football or finesse jig, fish it on 12 to 15 lb fluorocarbon, and drag it slowly with long pauses. If the water is in the 50s and the bass are showing signs of life, step up to the Strike King Rage Tail Chunk for a little more action. If the water is stained, switch to the Berkley MaxScent Meaty Chunk and let the scent do the work. If you are flipping cover, rig the Sweet Beaver or the Fat Baby Craw and give the bass time to find it.

The cold-water jig game rewards patience more than tackle, but the right trailer makes patience pay. A compact, low-action trailer that falls slowly and looks like an easy meal will catch bass all winter long, while a high-action trailer built for summer will get ignored. Buy the Cherry Bug first, keep a pack of Super Chunks in the box for the days when the fishing is snaggy, and save the Rage Craw for the day the water warms up and the bass start chasing again.
