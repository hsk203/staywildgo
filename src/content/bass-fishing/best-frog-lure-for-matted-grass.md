## What Matted Grass Demands From a Frog

Matted grass is not one kind of cover. Early in the season it is scattered milfoil and pondweed with open lanes between the clumps; by late summer it is a solid canopy of hydrilla or duckweed thick enough to walk on, with bass holding underneath it in the shade. BassResource's frog-fishing guide describes the prime water as large flats of matted vegetation, and the fish sit under the mat where the water is cooler and darker, listening for anything moving across the ceiling above them. A frog that works here has to do four things, and most cheap frogs fail at least one of them.

First, it has to stay weedless. The hook points must sit tight against the body so the frog slides over stems and pads instead of snagging, but the body must collapse easily when a bass closes its mouth, or the hooks never reach flesh. That balance — a soft, collapsible body with hooks buried just under the surface — is what separates a frog that catches fish from one that only catches blowups. The BOOYAH Pad Crasher earned its reputation on exactly this point: Wired2Fish's review of the Pad Crasher calls out its super-soft, collapsible body and sharp hooks as the reason its hookup ratio holds up in heavy cover.

Second, it has to walk. A frog that only swims straight is a frog that bass follow and ignore. The side-to-side "walk the dog" action — the frog zigzagging across the mat with each twitch of the rod tip — is what makes a bass commit, because a real frog never swims in a straight line. The shape of the belly matters here: a frog with a narrow, keeled belly walks with light rod twitches, while a flat, wide frog needs more effort and tends to plow. TackleTour's review of the SPRO Bronzeye Frog 65 notes that the bait's weighted belly keeps it landing upright and walking cleanly, which is why it has stayed the benchmark frog for two decades.

Third, it has to cast far enough to cover the flat. Matted grass often stretches for dozens of yards, and the fish could be anywhere under it. A frog under 1/2 oz struggles to carry a long cast on heavy braid, and a frog over 3/4 oz lands hard and spooks shallow fish. The 1/2 oz to 5/8 oz range is the sweet spot for most mat fishing, and it is no accident that four of the six frogs in this guide sit in that window.

Fourth, it has to survive the mat. A frog gets dragged across abrasive stems, chewed by bass, and yanked through vegetation on every retrieve. A body that tears after two fish is a false economy, even at a low price — which is why the cheapest frog on this list is a soft-plastic toad you expect to replace, while every hollow body here is built to last a season of hard use. Cast and Spear's 2026 frog roundup makes the same distinction, sorting its picks by build quality and hook design rather than by price alone.

## How to Fish a Frog Over Matted Grass

The retrieve matters more than the frog. The same frog fished three different ways over the same mat will get three different results, and most missed fish are a retrieve problem, not a lure problem. BassResource's frog-fishing guide lays out the method that works across all of these frogs, and it starts before the cast: fan-cast the flat. Make a cast to the 10 o'clock position, work it back, then cast to 11 o'clock, then 12, and so on — covering the mat in a careful, systematic pattern instead of bombing casts at random. Matted grass is a big, flat target, and the fish could be anywhere under it, so the angler who covers water methodically catches more than the angler who fishes the same ten yards all morning.

### Walking the frog

Walking the dog is the retrieve that makes a hollow-body frog work. With the rod tip down, give a short downward twitch, reel in the slack, and twitch again — each twitch kicks the frog to one side, and the alternating twitches walk it in a zigzag. The key detail, and the one beginners miss, is that the twitch comes from the wrist and the rod tip, not the arm. A big sweep pulls the frog too far and kills the cadence; short, crisp twitches keep it dancing. The frog should move six inches to a foot per twitch, with a pause of a second or two between sequences. On a mat, walk the frog across the canopy and pause it at every change: a hole, a torn edge, a darker patch, a point where the grass thins. BassResource's topwater frog article notes that bass often hear the frog working over the mat and blow through it to get the bait — and the strikes come most often during the pause, when the frog is sitting still in a hole and looks like the easiest meal under the mat.

### Punching through and dragging

On the thickest mats, walking is not enough — the frog has to get through the canopy, not just across it. This is where the heavier frogs earn their keep. Cast the LIVETARGET 3/4 oz frog or the Bronzeye 65 past the target, then drag it steadily across the mat, letting the weight push stems aside. Do not twitch hard on a solid mat; a hard twitch on thick grass just tangles the frog. Drag it slowly, stop it wherever the cover changes, and let it sit. The pause is the trigger: a frog sitting motionless in a hole in the mat is the most vulnerable-looking thing a bass buried underneath will see all day, and the strike usually comes two or three seconds into the pause, not during the pull.

### The pause and the hookset

The hookset is where frog fishing is won and lost, and it is the single biggest reason beginners lose fish on frogs. The blowup is spectacular — water erupts, the mat shakes, and every instinct says to swing immediately. Do not. BassResource's frog-fishing video is blunt about it: wait for the fish. The angler in the video waits and waits after the blowup until the line starts swimming off, then tightens down and sets the hook in one motion. The written guide on the same site says the same thing in plainer terms: give the fish a second or two after the splash before setting, and train yourself to wait until you feel the bait instead of just watching the explosion.

The mechanics matter as much as the timing. Keep a little slack in the line, drop the rod tip when the fish blows up, reel until the line comes tight and you feel solid weight, then set hard with a sweep to the side — BassResource describes it as cracking a whip, a single fast motion that drives the double hooks through the frog's body and into the fish's mouth. A straight overhead set pulls the frog up and away from the fish; a sideways sweep keeps the hooks moving into the jaw. And when the fish is on, keep reeling and keep the rod loaded — a bass buried in a mat will wrap you around stems in seconds if you give it slack, and the fight is won by winching the fish up and out of the cover, not by playing it gently. If you want the full mechanics on the rod and reel side, our guide to the [best baitcaster combo under $150](/bass-fishing/best-baitcaster-combo-under-150/) covers the reels that handle this kind of fishing, and the [baitcaster vs spinning reel](/bass-fishing/baitcaster-vs-spinning-reel/) comparison explains why a baitcaster is the right tool for heavy-cover frog work.

### When they blow up and miss

Bass miss frogs on mats constantly, and it is not always your fault. A bass striking up through a thick mat often cannot see the frog — it is striking at sound and vibration, and it blows through the canopy a foot to the side of the bait. When a fish blows up and misses, do not reel in and cast somewhere else. Leave the frog where it is for a few seconds — the fish often comes back for a second try — and if it does not, make a fast follow-up cast to the exact hole the blowup came from. BassResource's guide recommends keeping a Texas-rigged soft plastic ready as a follow-up bait: get the frog back in quickly, then pitch the Texas rig into the hole before the fish settles. The [spring bass fishing tips](/bass-fishing/spring-bass-fishing-tips/) guide covers the same follow-up thinking for the spring bite, and it applies all summer on the mat. A missed blowup is information, not failure — it tells you exactly where a fish is sitting, and a fish that has already shown itself once is the easiest fish on the lake to catch on the second cast.

## Frog Gear for Matted Grass: Rod, Reel, and Line

Frog fishing in matted grass is the most gear-dependent style in bass fishing, and the wrong setup loses fish that the right setup lands. The rod is the starting point. BassResource's frog-fishing guide uses a 7-foot-3-inch heavy rod with a fast tip, and says anything from 7 feet to 7 feet 8 inches works — the length gives casting distance across a wide mat, the heavy power gives the backbone to winch a fish out of the grass, and the fast tip loads up for long casts and drives the hook home. Wired2Fish's Pad Crasher review uses a 7-foot-2-inch heavy rod for the same reasons. A medium-heavy rod can work on sparse cover and open water, but on a solid mat it folds when you set the hook and again when the fish buries itself — and a folded rod is a lost fish.

The reel should be a baitcasting reel with a gear ratio around 7.1:1 or faster. The speed matters for two reasons: it picks up line fast enough to keep up with a fish swimming toward the boat after the strike, and it lets you reel the frog back in quickly for the next cast after a miss. Wired2Fish's Pad Crasher reviewer pairs the frog with an 8:1 reel for exactly this reason. A slower reel leaves slack in the line at the worst moment — right when you need to feel the weight of the fish before setting the hook.

The line is not negotiable: braided line, 50-pound test as the starting point, 65-pound for the thickest mats. Mono and fluorocarbon stretch, and that stretch kills hooksets through a hollow-body frog — the hook has to travel through the frog's body and into the fish's mouth, and any stretch in the line absorbs the force. Braid has near-zero stretch, and its thin diameter cuts through grass stems when a hooked fish tries to wrap you up. BassResource's guide fishes 50-pound braid specifically because heavier braid costs casting distance on long casts across a flat — 65-pound is the call when the cover is thick enough that landing the fish matters more than reaching the far edge. Either way, spool braid and nothing else; a frog on mono over a mat is a fish lost.

## Picking a Frog Color for the Water and the Sky

Frog color confuses anglers because they try to match the grass. The bass is not looking at the grass — it is looking up at the frog against the sky, and the frog's belly is what it sees. So the color rule is about the sky and the water, not the cover. On a bright, sunny day with a clear sky, a dark frog — black, dark green, or brown — silhouettes best against the light, and the bass sees a crisp shape. On a cloudy day or in low light at dawn and dusk, a white or chartreuse frog shows up better against a grey sky. In stained water, a brighter frog with a chartreuse or white belly is easier for the fish to track; in clear water, natural greens and browns look like the real frogs the bass eat every day.

That is the whole system, and it means you need three frogs, not thirty. A dark one, a white one, and a natural green one cover every sky and water combination on the mat. The 52 colors on the Bronzeye and the 14 patterns on the LIVETARGET are nice to have, but the fish does not care about the exact shade of green on the frog's back — it cares about the silhouette against the sky and the action across the mat. Spend the money on a second rod or better braid instead of a fourth color.

One more color note: on matted grass, the frog's legs matter as much as its body. White or chartreuse skirt legs flash on every twitch and help the bass track the frog across a dark mat; dark legs disappear against the canopy. If a frog comes with dark legs and you are fishing a dark mat on a bright day, trim the legs shorter — the shorter skirt walks tighter and shows less dark material against the grass. The [best chatterbait trailers](/bass-fishing/best-chatterbait-trailers/) guide makes the same point about matching trailer color to the water rather than the cover, and the logic is identical here.

## A Simple Plan for Your First Morning on the Mat

Start with the Bronzeye 65 in a natural green, on 50-pound braid, with a 7-foot-plus heavy rod. Fan-cast the flat from the outside edge inward, walking the frog with short twitches and pausing it in every hole. When a fish blows up, wait for the weight, then set hard to the side. If the mat is solid and the frog keeps skating across the top without drawing strikes, switch to the LIVETARGET 3/4 oz frog and drag it slowly, pausing longer. If the fish are following but not committing, drop down to the Pad Crasher or the Lunker Frog — the smaller, softer, more realistic profiles get bit when the big frog gets refused. And when the mat has holes in it, or the morning is calm, tie on the Poppin' Pad Crasher and pop it in the pockets. Keep the Horny Toad in the box for covering water fast and finding where the fish are holding, then come back to those spots with a hollow body.

Matted grass fishing is not subtle, and that is the appeal. The strikes are violent, the fight is a tug-of-war with the whole lake, and the fish that live under a mat in August are the biggest fish in the lake. The right frog, fished the right way, is the only lure that can reach them — and once you feel a bass blow up through a foot of grass and then feel the weight come tight on the line, no other kind of fishing feels quite as alive.
