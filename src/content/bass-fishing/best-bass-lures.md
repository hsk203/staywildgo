If you could own only one bass lure, the answer is the 5-inch Gary Yamamoto Senko: a salt-impregnated stickbait that falls with a slow, side-to-side shimmy on a weightless rig, and Tackle Warehouse lists it at $7–$9 for a 10-pack. But the Senko is only the start. Bass change depth, cover, and mood with the season and the water clarity, so the anglers who catch fish most consistently carry a small rotation that covers the surface, the mid-depths, and the bottom. ## The 8 Best Bass Lures

### 1. Best Overall Soft Plastic: Gary Yamamoto Senko (5-Inch Stickbait)

The Senko earns its reputation on the fall. Rigged weightless, the salt-heavy plastic sinks horizontally with a subtle shimmy that looks like a dying baitfish, and it does that with no rod movement at all. That is why it is the lure most guides hand to a beginner and the lure tournament anglers still tie on when the bite gets tough: it catches fish on days when nothing else gets a look. Fish it around docks, laydowns, weed edges, and pond banks, and let it sink on a slack line so the bait, not the rod, does the work.

**Specs:** Tackle Warehouse and Sportsman's Warehouse list the 5-inch Senko at $7–$9 per 10-pack as of November 2026. The bait is 5 inches long, comes 10 per pack, and Field & Stream's Senko guide lists the popular colors as Watermelon, Black, Green Pumpkin, and Bubble Gum. The 5-inch size is the all-purpose choice; the 4-inch version suits finesse presentations and the 6-inch version suits bigger water.

**Pros:**

- Catches bass in every season and almost every body of water
- Fishes well on both a wacky rig and a Texas rig
- Cheap per bait, at roughly 70 to 90 cents each

**Cons:**

- Soft plastic tears easily, so one bait rarely survives many fish
- Needs a slow, patient retrieve that some anglers find boring
- Sinks slowly, so it is a poor choice for covering deep water quickly

**Colors and setup:** Start with Green Pumpkin with Black Flake (stock number 9-10-297 on Tackle Warehouse) in clear water and Watermelon Red in stained water. For the wacky rig, hook the bait through the middle with a 1/0 or 2/0 wacky hook and an O-ring to stop tearing; our [how to rig a Senko](/bass-fishing/how-to-rig-a-senko/) guide walks through both rigs step by step. For the Texas rig, use a 3/0 offset worm hook with a 1/8 oz bullet weight. A 7-foot medium spinning rod with 12 lb fluorocarbon handles the wacky rig well, while a medium-heavy casting rod suits the Texas rig.

### 2. Best Bladed Jig (ChatterBait): Z-Man Evergreen JackHammer

When bass are buried in grass or the water turns to chocolate milk after a storm, the JackHammer is the lure that keeps producing. It was designed with Elite Series pro Brett Hite and Japanese lure maker Evergreen International, and its flat-bottom, low-center-of-gravity head and thin stainless blade start vibrating the instant you turn the reel handle. The blade makes the bait hunt side to side like a fleeing baitfish, and the thump carries through murky water where bass hunt by feel. It is the most expensive lure on this list by a wide margin, and it is also the one anglers reach for when a tournament check is on the line.

**Specs:** Sportsman's Warehouse lists the JackHammer at $19.99–$21.99 depending on size, and the 3/8 oz and 1/2 oz sizes are the most common choices. MonsterBass lists the hook as a custom 5/0 Gamakatsu, and the bait ships with a Decoy Egg Snap and a double wire trailer keeper. The head uses a low-center-of-gravity design with 3D eyes, and the blade is a thin stainless hex-shaped ChatterBlade.

**Pros:**

- Starts vibrating immediately, even on a slow retrieve
- Stays down and tracks straight at high retrieve speeds
- Premium hook and snap are ready for big fish out of the package

**Cons:**

- At $19–$22, it costs three times a standard bladed jig
- Losing one to a snag stings more than losing a $7–$9 crankbait
- The premium price buys refinement, not a guarantee of bites

**Colors and setup:** Brett's Bluegill and Green Pumpkin Shad are the two patterns to own first; Sportsman's Warehouse lists both among the 16 available colors. Add a trailer to change the profile: Wired2Fish names the 4-inch Yamamoto Zako its best overall ChatterBait trailer, and our [best ChatterBait trailers](/bass-fishing/best-chatterbait-trailers/) guide compares the top options. Fish the 3/8 oz size in 3–8 feet of water over grass, and step up to 1/2 oz when the wind picks up or you need a longer cast. A 7-foot to 7-foot-3 medium-heavy casting rod with 15–17 lb fluorocarbon is the standard setup.

### 3. Best Topwater Lure: River2Sea Whopper Plopper 90 / 110

Few moments in bass fishing match a big fish blowing up on a Whopper Plopper at first light. The rotating soft tail churns the surface into a gurgling wake that bass can hear and see from a long way off, and the steady retrieve means there is almost nothing to learn: cast it out, reel it back at a steady pace, and wait for the explosion. It shines in the low light of dawn and dusk, in the fall when bass chase baitfish on the surface, and anywhere bass are feeding up high.

**Specs:** Bushlife's size chart lists the 90 at 3-1/2 inches and 1/2 oz and the 110 at 4-3/8 inches and 1 oz, and Outdoor Life's smallmouth guide agrees on the 110 at 4-3/8 inches and 1 oz, naming Loon, Bone, and Monkey Butt among the key colors. Street prices run $13–$18 as of November 2026. The 90 suits calm ponds and smaller water; the 110 covers bigger water and draws bigger fish.

**Pros:**

- Dead-simple retrieve that works for a first-time topwater angler
- Loud surface disturbance calls fish up from depth
- Two sizes cover ponds through large reservoirs

**Cons:**

- Two exposed treble hooks snag in heavy vegetation
- Nearly useless in thick mats or heavy slop
- The steady retrieve can feel one-dimensional on slow days

**Colors and setup:** Loon is the default in low light and stained water because its dark silhouette reads clearly against the sky; Bone is the pick for clear water and bright sun. Tie on a small snap or swivel to keep the spinning tail from twisting your line, and fish it on a 7-foot medium-heavy casting rod with 30–40 lb braided line. Braid floats, which keeps the bait on top, and it has no stretch, which helps drive the trebles home. Pause for a beat after the blowup before setting the hook — bass often miss on the first swipe and come back.

### 4. Best Spinnerbait for Murky Water: BOOYAH Blade Spinnerbait

A spinnerbait is the lure you tie on when the wind is howling, the water is stained, and you need to cover water without hanging up every cast. The wire frame deflects off wood and rock, the skirt pulses as it comes through, and the spinning blades throw flash and vibration that bass track in low visibility. The BOOYAH Blade is the workhorse version: affordable, durable, and available in the blade combinations that matter most for stained water.

**Specs:** Target lists the BOOYAH Double Colorado Blade spinnerbait in 3/8 oz at $12–$13, with a 4/0 Mustad hook and a 55-strand silicone skirt. CamelCamelCamel records an Amazon list price of $12–$13 for the 1/2 oz Double Willow model in White Chartreuse. The 3/8 oz size is the most versatile for bank and boat fishing; step up to 1/2 oz for wind, deeper water, or longer casts.

**Pros:**

- Comes through wood, rock, and sparse grass without hanging up
- Blades create vibration bass can follow in murky water
- One lure covers shallow flats, points, and wind-blown banks

**Cons:**

- Less effective than a jig or soft plastic when bass are tight to the bottom
- Skirts wear out and need replacing after heavy use
- The wire arm can bend after a hard snag or a big fish

**Colors and setup:** In stained water, start with chartreuse and white with a gold Colorado blade; Outdoor Life's spinnerbait guide recommends exactly that combination for dirty water, with gold Colorado blades for thump and chartreuse, black and blue, or purple skirts for visibility. In clear water, switch to white with a silver willow blade. Our [best spinnerbait color for muddy water in spring](/bass-fishing/best-spinnerbait-color-for-muddy-water-in-spring/) guide breaks down the color rules by season. Fish it on a 6-foot-6 to 7-foot medium-heavy casting rod with 14–17 lb fluorocarbon, and keep the retrieve slow and steady in murk so bass can track the vibration.

### 5. Best Squarebill Crankbait: Strike King KVD 1.5

The KVD 1.5 is the crankbait every other squarebill is measured against. Its short square lip lets it crash into stumps, rocks, and dock posts, then kick sideways in an erratic deflection that triggers reaction strikes from bass holding tight to cover. Kevin VanDam designed the bait, and it won him the 2011 Bassmaster Classic in the Louisiana Delta. Fish it where other crankbaits fear to go: through laydowns, over riprap, and along shallow rock banks in 3–6 feet of water.

**Specs:** Tackle Warehouse lists the KVD Elite 1.5 at 2-1/4 inches, 3/8 oz, and a 3–6 foot diving depth. MidwayUSA lists the KVD 1.5 Shallow Square Bill at $9–$10. The standard KVD 1.5 comes in rattling and silent versions; the silent version is the pick for heavily pressured water where bass have heard every rattle in the lake.

**Pros:**

- Deflects off cover instead of snagging, which is the whole point of a squarebill
- Triggers reaction strikes from bass that ignore a straight retrieve
- The 3–6 foot range covers the most productive shallow zone

**Cons:**

- Limited to shallow water; it cannot reach fish holding deeper than 6 feet
- Treble hooks grab grass and demand a clean retrieve lane
- The lip can chip after repeated impacts on rock

**Colors and setup:** Sexy Shad and Chartreuse Sexy Shad are the two patterns to start with — Sexy Shad for clear water with shad forage, Chartreuse Sexy Shad when the water has a stain. When the water turns truly muddy, our [best bass lures for murky water](/bass-fishing/best-bass-lures-for-murky-water/) guide recommends switching to a chartreuse-and-black-back squarebill. Fish it on a 7-foot medium casting rod with 12 lb fluorocarbon, and crank it straight into cover on purpose: the deflection, not the straight swim, is what gets bit.

### 6. Best Lipless Crankbait: Bill Lewis Rat-L-Trap / Strike King Red Eye Shad (1/2 oz)

When bass are scattered and you need to find them fast, a lipless crankbait is the search tool. It sinks, so you control the depth, and its tight vibration and loud rattle call fish in from a distance. Burn it across shallow grass flats in spring, yo-yo it off the bottom in fall when bass chase schools of shad, or rip it free of grass and let it flutter down on a slack line — the strike usually comes on the fall.

**Specs:** MidwayUSA lists the original Bill Lewis Rat-L-Trap in 1/2 oz at $6–$7, with a 3-inch body. Tackle Warehouse lists the Strike King Red Eye Shad in 1/2 oz at $9–$10, in colors including Chrome Sexy Shad, Chrome Black Back, and Natural Shad. The 1/2 oz size is the standard for both baits; the Rat-L-Trap also comes in 1/4, 3/4, and 1 oz sizes for shallower or deeper work.

**Pros:**

- Covers water faster than any soft plastic or jig
- Sinks to any depth, so one bait fishes the whole column
- The rattle and tight wobble trigger reaction strikes from inactive fish

**Cons:**

- Two trebles snag in thick grass and heavy wood
- Easy to fish too fast and pull it away from following bass
- Losing one to a snag is common when ripping through cover

**Colors and setup:** Chrome Blue Back and Chrome Black Back are the classic clear-water choices; Red Crawfish is the spring pattern when bass key on crawfish in warming shallows. For the retrieve, cast long, let the bait sink to the depth you want, then reel with a steady crank and sharp rod pops — the yo-yo retrieve. A 7-foot medium-heavy casting rod with 12–15 lb fluorocarbon gives you the casting distance and the sensitivity to feel the bait tick grass. ### 7. Best Hollow Body Frog for Heavy Cover: BOOYAH Pad Crasher

Some of the biggest bass of the summer live where no other lure can go: under matted lily pads and tangled surface vegetation. The Pad Crasher is built for exactly that water. Its hollow, collapsible body and weedless double hook slide across the mat, and when a bass blows up on it, the body collapses and the hooks find flesh. Walk it across the pads with short twitches, pause it in the open pockets, and wait for the explosion.

**Specs:** Sportsman's Warehouse lists the Pad Crasher at $9–$10, with a 2-1/2 inch body and a 1/2 oz weight. Target lists the same bait at $12–$13 in Bullfrog, Leopard Frog, and Shad Frog colors, and confirms the 3/0 double hook. The 1/2 oz weight gives it the casting distance a frog needs to reach the far edge of a mat from the bank.

**Pros:**

- Fishes mats and pads that snag every other lure
- Collapsible body gives a strong hookup ratio for a frog
- The 1/2 oz weight casts far, even into a headwind

**Cons:**

- Hookup ratio is still lower than a single-hook soft plastic
- Needs heavy tackle, which makes it a poor choice for light spinning gear
- The bite often comes on the pause, which tests your patience

**Colors and setup:** Shad Frog matches open-water baitfish, Leopard Frog matches natural frogs around pads, and Bullfrog is the dark silhouette for low light. The hookset is the skill that matters most: when a bass blows up, wait two seconds — count them — before setting the hook, or you will pull the frog out of the fish's mouth. Fish it on a 7-foot to 7-foot-4 heavy casting rod with 50–65 lb braided line and no leader; braid has no stretch, which is what drives the double hook through the frog's body and into the fish. ### 8. Best Football Jig for Deep Structure: Strike King Tour Grade Football Jig

When summer heat or winter cold pushes bass off the bank and onto deep rock piles, ledges, and points in 15–25 feet of water, a football jig is the tool that stays in contact with the bottom and finds them. The wide football head rocks and stands the bait upright on the bottom, the skirt breathes with each drag, and a soft-plastic trailer adds the movement that seals the deal. It is slow fishing, and it is how many of the biggest bass of the year get caught.

**Specs:** MidwayUSA lists the Tour Grade Football Jig at $5–$6, and Tackle Warehouse lists the same range. MidwayUSA lists a 3/8 oz model in Black Blue, and the jig comes in 1/2 oz and 3/4 oz sizes for deeper water. The hook is an extra-sharp Gamakatsu 60-degree round bend, and the flat-eye line tie keeps the knot in the right position so the jig tracks true on the drag.

**Pros:**

- Holds bottom contact on deep rock better than any other jig shape
- The football head stands the bait up, which keeps the trailer visible
- At $5–$6, it is the cheapest lure on this list

**Cons:**

- Slow fishing that covers far less water than a crankbait
- Needs a sensitive rod to feel subtle bottom changes and light bites
- Easy to snag in heavy wood, where a flipping jig works better

**Colors and setup:** Black Blue is the murky-water and low-light choice; Green Pumpkin is the clear-water and rock-pile choice. Pair it with a craw-style trailer such as a Strike King Rage Craw, matching the trailer color to the skirt. Fish it on a 7-foot-3 to 7-foot-6 medium-heavy to heavy casting rod with 14–17 lb fluorocarbon — fluorocarbon sinks and stays sensitive at depth, and our [best fluorocarbon line for bass fishing](/bass-fishing/best-fluorocarbon-line-for-bass-fishing/) guide covers which lines hold up best. Drag it slowly across the bottom, pause, and watch for the line to jump or go slack.

## How to Choose the Right Bass Lure

### Matching Lure Color to Water Clarity

Water clarity decides color before anything else. In clear water, where you can see the bottom in more than 3 feet of water, bass hunt by sight and notice details, so match the local forage with natural colors: Green Pumpkin, Watermelon, and Ghost Shad. In stained water, with 1–3 feet of visibility, add some flash — chartreuse and white, or a chrome-sided crankbait. In muddy water, with less than a foot of visibility, bass hunt by vibration and silhouette, so switch to high-contrast colors: Black and Blue, or Chartreuse and White. The formula is simple: the dirtier the water, the louder the color and the stronger the vibration.

### Seasonal Bass Lure Chart

| Season | Water Temp | First-Choice Lures |
|---|---|---|
| Spring | 55–65°F | Senko, spinnerbait, squarebill — shallow flats, spawning pockets |
| Summer | 70–85°F | Frog, football jig, topwater — grass edges, deep ledges, mats |
| Fall | 55–70°F | Lipless crankbait, spinnerbait — points, baitfish schools |
| Winter | 40–50°F | Football jig, suspending jerkbait — deep structure, channel bends |

Water temperatures are typical ranges for largemouth bass, not hard rules — a warm spring or a cold snap shifts everything by a week or two. The pattern to remember is that bass follow baitfish and comfort: shallow and aggressive in spring and fall, deep or under cover in the heat of summer and the cold of winter.

### Recommended Rod, Reel, and Line Setup

You do not need eight rods for eight lures. Three cover the list: a 7-foot medium spinning rod with 12 lb fluorocarbon for the Senko and other finesse presentations; a 7-foot medium-heavy casting rod with 15 lb fluorocarbon for the JackHammer, squarebill, lipless crankbait, and football jig; and a 7-foot heavy casting rod with 50 lb braid for the frog. The frog setup is non-negotiable — braid is the only line with the strength and zero stretch to pull a big bass out of a mat. For the rest, fluorocarbon sinks and stays nearly invisible underwater, which matters most in clear water.
