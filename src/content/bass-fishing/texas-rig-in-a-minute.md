The Texas rig is the workhorse of bass fishing: weedless, simple, and effective in almost any cover. Tie it with a Palomar knot, a 3/0 or 4/0 EWG worm hook, and a bullet weight matched to the depth — 1/8 oz for shallow water, 1/4 to 3/8 oz for most fishing, and 1/2 oz or more for deep water and heavy grass. Thread the weight on first, tie the hook on, then rig the soft plastic so the point is buried and the bait hangs straight. Fish it slowly along the bottom, reel down to tighten the line when you feel a bite, and then set the hook.

That is the whole rig in one paragraph. The rest of this guide fills in the details that make the difference between a rig that catches fish and one that just gets wet: the knot sequence step by step, how to match hook and weight to the bait, when to peg the weight and when to let it slide, the retrieves that work, and the two mistakes that cost beginners the most fish. Tackle Warehouse's Texas rig guide puts the standard weight range at 1/4 to 3/8 oz for most situations, with 1/8 oz for shallow water and 1/2 oz for deeper water and heavier cover, and the standard hook at 3/0 or 4/0 — the same sizes this guide uses throughout.

The Texas rig is the first rig in the [best bass lures](/bass-fishing/best-bass-lures/) rotation for a reason, and it pairs naturally with a [wacky-rigged Senko](/bass-fishing/how-to-rig-a-senko/) when the fish want a slower fall. If you fish ponds, the same rig leads the [best bass lures for ponds](/bass-fishing/best-bass-lures-for-ponds/) list.

## What you need

A Texas rig has only three parts, and that is the point. Nothing about it is complicated, which is why it is the first rig most bass anglers learn and the one they keep coming back to for the rest of their fishing lives. The parts are a bullet weight, a worm hook, and a soft plastic bait. Line matters too: most anglers fish a Texas rig on 12 to 17 lb fluorocarbon or on 30 to 50 lb braid with a fluorocarbon leader, and the [best fluorocarbon line for bass fishing](/bass-fishing/best-fluorocarbon-line-for-bass-fishing/) guide covers how to choose between them. Fluorocarbon sinks and stays nearly invisible underwater; braid gives you maximum sensitivity and hook-setting power in heavy cover.

| Component | What to buy | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Bullet weight | 1/8 to 3/8 oz tungsten or lead | Controls fall rate and bottom contact |
| Worm hook | 3/0 or 4/0 EWG, or offset round bend | Hook gap must clear the plastic on the set |
| Soft plastic | Stick bait, creature bait, straight-tail worm | The bait is the profile the bass sees |

Tungsten weights cost more than lead — a pack of tungsten bullet weights typically runs about $6 to $10 at Tackle Warehouse as of November 2026 — but tungsten is denser, so a 1/4 oz tungsten weight is smaller than the same weight in lead, slips through grass more easily, and transmits bottom contact more clearly. Lead is cheaper and perfectly serviceable; start with lead and switch to tungsten once you know which sizes you use most. Either way, buy weights in a few sizes rather than one: 1/8, 3/16, 1/4, and 3/8 oz covers nearly everything a Texas rig is asked to do.

## The Palomar knot, step by step

The Palomar is the knot to learn first. It is strong, it is hard to tie wrong, and it works with mono, fluorocarbon, and braid. Field & Stream's machine pull tests put the Palomar at or near the top for terminal connections — 89 percent of 10 lb mono and 82 percent of fluorocarbon — because the doubled line through the hook eye spreads the load across two strands instead of one. It takes under a minute once your hands know the sequence, and it is the knot this guide uses for every Texas rig.

### Step 1: Double the line

Pull about 6 to 8 inches of line off the reel and fold it back on itself to make a loop. You are working with a doubled line from here on, which is what gives the Palomar its strength. Keep the two strands parallel and do not pinch a hard crease into the line, since a kink is a weak spot later.

### Step 2: Pass the loop through the eye

Push the doubled loop through the eye of the hook. Pull enough through so that 3 to 4 inches of loop hangs past the eye, with the hook dangling below. If the hook eye is small, pass the tag end through the eye once, then pass it back through from the other direction to create the same doubled section.

### Step 3: Tie a loose overhand knot

Using the doubled loop on one side and the main line and tag end on the other, tie a simple overhand knot. Keep it loose. The hook should hang in the middle of the knot, below it, and the loop needs to stay large enough to pass over the whole hook in the next step. Do not tighten anything yet — tightening early traps the loop against the eye and weakens the knot.

### Step 4: Pass the hook through the loop

Open the remaining loop wide and pass the entire hook through it — point, bend, and all. The loop has to clear the hook point completely and settle around the connection above the eye. This is the one step that trips people up: if the loop is too small, go back to Step 3 and leave more loop. A hook that will not fit through the loop is the Palomar's one real limitation, and it is why the knot is less handy for very large lures.

### Step 5: Wet the knot and cinch it down

Wet the knot with water or saliva before pulling it tight. Dry friction heats and weakens mono and fluorocarbon at exactly the point where the knot needs to be strongest. Then pull the standing line and the tag end slowly and evenly until the knot seats snugly against the top of the hook eye. Watch the wraps as they seat: if they cross or bunch, back off and retie.

### Step 6: Trim the tag and pull-check it

Trim the tag end to about 1/8 inch, leaving a small tag rather than cutting it flush — especially with braid, which can slip if the tag is too short. Then give the knot a firm pull with both hands before you cast. A knot that is going to fail should fail now, at the bank, not on a fish.

## The Uni knot alternative

The Palomar is the default, but the Uni knot is worth knowing as a backup, and many anglers prefer it for fluorocarbon because it seats more gradually. To tie it, pass the tag end through the hook eye and pull 6 to 8 inches through, then fold the tag back so it runs parallel to the standing line. Form a loop by bringing the tag back toward the eye, then wrap the tag around both parallel strands and through the loop five or six times, working from the eye outward — six wraps for thin line and braid, four or five for heavy mono above about 20 lb. Wet the wraps, pull the tag to draw them into a neat barrel, then pull the standing line to slide the barrel down against the eye and trim the tag. The Uni uses more line than the Palomar and takes a few seconds longer, but it works on hooks and lures that are too bulky to pass through a Palomar loop, and it doubles as a line-to-line connection in its double-Uni form. Take Me Fishing's knot guide lists the same five-to-six-wrap count, so either source agrees on the method.

## Matching hook size to bait size

The hook has to match the bait. Too small and the plastic balls up in the bend and blocks the point on the hookset; too big and the bait looks stiff and unnatural. Tackle Warehouse's guide gives the rule plainly: 3/0 or 4/0 offset or EWG for most baits, down to 1/0 or 2/0 for finesse baits, and up to 5/0 or 6/0 for large soft plastics. The table below follows that rule and adds the bait lengths that go with each size.

| Hook size | Best for | Bait length |
|---|---|---|
| 1/0 to 2/0 | Finesse worms, small craws | Under 5 inches |
| 3/0 | Straight-tail worms, lizards, flukes | 5 to 6 inches |
| 4/0 | Creature baits, beaver baits, big craws | 6 to 7 inches |
| 5/0 | Oversized creature baits, 10-inch worms | 7 inches and up |

Hook style matters as much as size. An offset round-bend hook suits skinny worms and finesse baits, because the slim profile keeps the bait straight. An EWG hook — extra wide gap — suits bulky creature baits and thick craws, because the wide gap leaves room for the plastic to collapse out of the way when the hook point drives home. For punching thick matted grass, anglers switch to a heavy-wire straight-shank hook, which stands up to the force of driving a big weight and a bulky bait through a mat. Keep 2/0, 3/0, and 4/0 in the box and you are covered for nearly every Texas rig situation; Gamakatsu's EWG offset worm hooks run from 1/0 to 5/0, and a pack typically costs about $4 to $6 at Tackle Warehouse as of November 2026.

## Matching weight size to depth and cover

The weight controls how fast the bait falls, how well you feel the bottom, and whether the rig gets through cover at all. The rule is simple: use the lightest weight that still lets you feel the bottom and keep contact with the bait. A lighter weight falls more slowly, and most Texas rig bites come on the fall, on slack line — so going lighter usually means more bites, as long as you can still tell what the bait is doing.

| Weight | Best use |
|---|---|
| 1/16 to 1/8 oz | Shallow water under 5 feet, sparse cover, slow fall |
| 3/16 to 1/4 oz | The everyday range, 5 to 15 feet, light grass |
| 3/8 oz | Deeper water, wind, moderate cover |
| 1/2 oz | Heavy cover, deep water, current |
| 3/4 to 1 oz and up | Punching thick matted grass |

Wind is the factor beginners forget. On a windy day, a 1/8 oz weight gets blown around and you lose contact with the bait; stepping up to 1/4 or 3/8 oz restores the feel. Current does the same thing. Depth does too: the deeper the water, the more weight you need to keep the bait down and the line tight enough to detect a bite. In thick matted vegetation, the weight has to be heavy enough to punch through the mat at all — 3/4 to 1 oz is normal there, and some anglers go to 1 1/2 oz on the heaviest mats. That is a specialized application, though; for most lakes and ponds, 1/4 oz is the weight you will reach for most often.

## Rigging the bait so it hangs straight

With the weight on the line and the hook tied on, the last step is threading the bait. Push the hook point into the head of the bait about a quarter inch, thread it through, and bring it out the side. Slide the bait up to the eye of the hook, rotate the hook, and bury the point back into the body of the bait so the rig is weedless. The bait should hang straight — if it curls or kinks, re-rig it. A crooked bait spins on the retrieve, and bass notice a spinning bait the way you would notice a wobbling wheel.

Check the rig from the side and from above before you cast. The hook point should be just under the skin of the plastic, with a tiny bit of the point barely breaking the surface or fully buried — either way, it should not stick out where it can snag grass. At the same time, the point should not be buried so deep that the plastic blocks it on the hookset. The sweet spot is a point that is hidden but only a thin layer of plastic away from daylight. With bulky creature baits, use an EWG hook so the gap stays open; with a straight-tail worm, an offset round bend keeps the slim profile straight. The same straight-and-weedless principle applies to a stick bait fished weightless — [how to rig a Senko](/bass-fishing/how-to-rig-a-senko/) covers the wacky and Texas-style versions of that rig. If you are new to this, practice at home with one bait until the motion is automatic — it takes about five minutes of repetition to lock in.

## Bait choices: stick baits, creatures, and worms

Three bait families cover nearly all Texas rig fishing, and each one has a job. A stick bait — a straight, salt-impregnated soft plastic in the 4 to 6 inch range — is the most versatile Texas rig bait there is. Rigged weightless or on a light 1/16 oz weight, it falls with a slow, shimmying action that bass find hard to ignore, and it is the bait to reach for when fish are finicky. A creature bait, with flapping appendages and a bulky body, displaces more water and works best in stained water and heavy cover, where the extra vibration helps bass find it. A straight-tail worm is the classic: a 6 to 7 inch worm on a 3/0 or 4/0 hook, fished slowly along the bottom, is the presentation that has caught bass for decades. For the full picture of which soft plastics belong in the box, the [best bass lures](/bass-fishing/best-bass-lures/) guide ranks the core options by situation.

Color follows the same rule as every other bass lure: match the water. In clear water, greens, browns, and natural shad patterns look like the real thing. In stained water, watermelon red and green pumpkin with red flake show up better. In muddy water, black and blue or Junebug — a dark purple with green flake — creates the strongest silhouette. None of this is complicated, and none of it needs a hundred colors: five or six proven colors in each bait family is a complete Texas rig box.

## Pegged or free-sliding: when each wins

The bullet weight on a Texas rig slides freely on the line unless you stop it, and that sliding is a feature. When the bait is falling on slack line, a free-sliding weight lets the bait fall naturally, fluttering down on its own while the weight drops a fraction faster — and most bites happen on that fall. A sliding weight also lets a bass pick up the bait and move off with it without immediately feeling the weight, which means the fish holds on longer and gives you more time to react.

Pegging the weight — fixing it tight against the hook eye with a rubber bobber stop, a peg, or a short piece of toothpick — makes sense in two situations. First, punching thick grass: a pegged weight drives the whole rig through the mat as one unit instead of the weight punching through and the bait hanging up above it. Second, fishing heavy wood and laydowns, where a sliding weight can wedge into a fork and leave the bait dangling. In open water, on flats, and around scattered cover, leave the weight free. It is worth repeating because it is the single most common rigging error: pegging the weight when you do not need to costs you bites on the fall for no benefit at all.

## Retrieves: drag, hop, and deadstick

There are three retrieves that cover nearly all Texas rig fishing, and they differ mainly in speed and patience. The drag is the default: cast out, let the bait fall to the bottom on slack line, then drag it slowly back with the rod, keeping the line just tight enough to feel the weight ticking over rocks and stumps. Reel in the slack as you go, but let the rod do the moving. The hop is the aggressive version: lift the rod tip a foot or two, let the bait fall back on slack line, and repeat. The hop works when bass are active and when you are fishing a slope or a weed edge, because the bait keeps re-entering the strike zone. The deadstick is the patient one: cast to a likely spot, let the bait settle, and do nothing for 10 to 30 seconds. It sounds like doing nothing, and it is — but a motionless stick bait on the bottom is one of the most effective presentations in cold water and on pressured fish, because the bait's own salt and scent do the work.

Speed is the variable that matters most. In water under 55°F, slow everything down: longer pauses, shorter hops, and more deadstick time. In water above 65°F, bass chase more willingly and a faster hop-and-drag retrieves more fish per hour. The retrieve also changes with cover: drag through open bottom and scattered grass, hop over rock and wood, deadstick around docks and isolated cover where a fish is likely holding tight. If you are not getting bites, change the retrieve before you change the bait — speed and pause are the two cheapest adjustments in fishing.

## Detecting the bite and setting the hook

A Texas rig bite rarely feels like a yank. More often it feels like a light tick, a sudden slackness in the line, or the weight of something that was not there a second ago. Sometimes the line simply starts moving sideways. That subtlety is why sensitivity matters: fluorocarbon transmits more feel than mono, and a graphite rod in the 7-foot medium-heavy range is the standard Texas rig rod for a reason. Watch the line as well as feel it — a line that jumps, twitches, or moves sideways is a fish even when your hands feel nothing.

When you feel the bite, do not set the hook immediately. Reel down until the line is tight and you feel the weight of the fish, then set the hook with a firm sweep of the rod, not a wild overhead jerk. Setting the hook while there is still slack in the line is the most common reason beginners miss fish on a Texas rig: the hook point never gets the force it needs to drive through the plastic and into the jaw. The reel-down takes about a second, and that second is the difference between a hookup and a missed bite. Once the fish is on, keep the rod loaded and the line tight, and reel steadily — a Texas rig fish in cover will head straight for the thickest stuff it can find, and slack line is how it gets there.

## Mistakes that cost beginners fish

The first mistake is pegging the weight when you do not need to, covered above: a free-sliding weight fishes better in open water. The second is setting the hook too early, also covered above: reel down first. The third is fishing too fast. A Texas rig is a slow presentation by design, and reeling it back like a crankbait wastes its strengths. If you are covering water quickly and not getting bites, slow down before you switch lures — the fish are often right where you already fished, just unwilling to chase a fast bait.

The fourth mistake is a crooked bait. Check the rig after every fish, every snag, and every few casts. A bait that has slid down the hook or twisted sideways will spin, and a spinning bait catches nothing. Re-rig it: it takes 20 seconds. The fifth mistake is using too heavy a weight. If you cannot feel the bottom, you need more weight; if you can feel the bottom clearly, you already have enough, and adding more only makes the bait fall faster and look less natural. The lightest weight that keeps bottom contact is the right weight, every time.
