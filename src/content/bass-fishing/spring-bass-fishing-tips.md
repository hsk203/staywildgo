Spring bass follow water temperature, not the calendar. The whole season runs on one number: when the water climbs from the mid-40s into the low 60s, bass leave their winter spots, feed hard on staging points, move onto the flats to spawn, and then slide back out to recover. The angler who tracks that temperature — and fishes the phase the water says the fish are in — catches bass all spring long, while the angler who fishes by the date keeps showing up a week early or a week late. This guide gives you the temperature table, the three phases, the lure rotation for each one, and the adjustments for cold fronts and bed fishing, so you can find fish before the spawn instead of guessing where they went.

The three phases are pre-spawn, spawn, and post-spawn, and they overlap all spring long: on the same lake in the same week, some fish are still staging deep while others are already on beds. That overlap is why the temperature matters more than any single rule — it tells you which phase most of the fish are in, and therefore where to start. For the post-spawn deep dive, once the fish have left the beds, see [spring bass after the spawn](/bass-fishing/spring-bass-after-the-spawn/).

## The water temperature table

The table below is the core of spring bass fishing. It comes from the temperature bands that FishUSA, Fishing Fathom, and other spring guides agree on, anchored by Wisconsin's Department of Natural Resources, which records largemouth bass choosing nest sites when the water reaches 60°F and laying eggs at 62 to 65°F. Read it as a set of bands to start in, not as exact lines — the bands shift with latitude, and southern lakes run weeks ahead of northern ones — but the order never changes.

| Water temperature | What the bass are doing | Where to find them |
|---|---|---|
| 45–50°F | Leaving winter spots, first move shallow | Main-lake points, creek-channel bends, 8–15 ft |
| 50–58°F | Pre-spawn feeding binge | First drop-offs off spawning flats, 4–10 ft |
| 58–60°F | Staging on the flats, males scouting nests | Flats and coves, 2–6 ft |
| 60–65°F | Nest building and peak spawning | Hard-bottom flats, gravel and sand, 1–6 ft |
| 65°F and up | Spawn winds down, post-spawn recovery | Deep edges off the beds, nearest cover, 8–15 ft |

Measure the temperature at the depth you fish, not at the surface. A surface reading in a shallow bay on a sunny afternoon can run several degrees warmer than the water three feet down, and the fish live in the cooler layer. A floating thermometer or the temperature reading on a fish finder gives you the number that matters, and checking it at the start of the day and again at midday tells you whether the fish are moving up or holding still. The trend matters as much as the reading: a stretch of three or four warm days pushes bass surprisingly shallow, while a cold front shoves them back out. Fish the trend, not the forecast.

## Pre-spawn: the best big-fish window of the year

The pre-spawn is the phase most spring anglers fish hardest, and for good reason: it holds the biggest bass of the year, and they are feeding. The phase begins when the water reaches the mid-40s and the fish start leaving their winter spots — deep structure, channel bends, and main-lake points in 8 to 15 feet of water — and it runs until the water hits the low 60s and the fish move onto the flats. Through that whole window, the females are packing on weight for the spawn, and they feed aggressively enough that a well-placed lure gets eaten out of irritation as often as hunger.

Early pre-spawn, in water from 45 to about 52°F, the fish are still deep and still slow. They hold on main-lake points and creek-channel bends, suspending over 8 to 15 feet of water, and they want a lure that stays in front of them without moving fast. A suspending jerkbait, worked with long pauses between twitches, is the classic early pre-spawn lure: it hangs motionless in the strike zone, and a cold bass that would ignore a fast bait will eat a jerkbait that simply sits there. A jig crawled slowly along the bottom is the second option, and a lipless crankbait ripped through staging areas is the third — its vibration calls fish from a distance in cold, stained water, and its fast retrieve covers the points and bends where the fish are stacked.

As the water crosses the low 50s and climbs toward 58°F, the fish move up to 4 to 10 feet and the bite speeds up. This is the pre-spawn feeding binge, and it is the best time of the year to catch a big bass. The fish are on the first drop-offs off the spawning flats, on secondary points inside creek arms, and around the cover — laydowns, stumps, rock — that sits just outside the flats. The lure rotation widens: add a shallow crankbait that deflects off cover, a spinnerbait for wind-blown points, and a bladed jig for stained water. The [best bass lures](/bass-fishing/best-bass-lures/) guide ranks these same four lures at the top of its pre-spawn list, and the [best chatterbait trailers](/bass-fishing/best-chatterbait-trailers/) guide covers the trailer choices that make a bladed jig work in cold, stained water. The retrieves get faster too, because the fish are now willing to chase. FishUSA's spring guide lists jerkbaits, spinnerbaits, lipless crankbaits, and jigs as the pre-spawn staples, and that list holds up across most of the country.

The key skill in the pre-spawn is finding the staging areas, and they are the same on nearly every lake: the last deep water before the spawning flats. A secondary point inside a creek arm, a channel bend that swings close to a flat, a hump or a ledge that sits between the wintering area and the spawning bay — these are the spots where pre-spawn bass stack up, and they are the spots worth fishing thoroughly before you ever look at the flats themselves. Work a point from deep to shallow, starting outside and moving in, and let the temperature tell you how deep to start.

## The spawn: beds, sight fishing, and the lures that work

When the water reaches about 60°F, the males move onto hard-bottomed flats and start fanning out nests — clean, light-colored circles on gravel, sand, or firm mud, in 1 to 6 feet of water. Peak spawning comes at 62 to 65°F, the range Wisconsin DNR records for egg laying, and the whole spawning window runs from the low 60s into the low 70s depending on the lake and the latitude. The males build the nests, the females visit to lay eggs and then leave, and the males stay to guard the eggs and then the fry. On a clear lake, the nests are visible from the boat as pale circles on the bottom, and that visibility is what makes the spawn the most visual phase of bass fishing.

Polarized sunglasses are the single most important piece of spawn-season equipment, and they matter more than any lure. A good pair of polarized lenses cuts the surface glare and lets you see into the water — the nests, the fish on them, and the bottom composition — in a way that bare eyes simply cannot. Without them, you are fishing blind on the flats; with them, you can pick out a bed at 30 feet, see whether a fish is on it, and place a cast where it counts. Amber or copper lenses are the usual choice for the stained water of spring, and a wraparound frame that blocks light from the sides works better than a flat fashion frame.

The lures for the spawn are slow and close to the bed. A lizard or a creature bait dragged slowly through the nest reads as an egg-eating intruder, and the guarding male attacks it out of defense rather than hunger — which is why the bite on a bed is often a hard, committed strike. A wacky-rigged stick bait, fished weightless and left to sit on the bed, works the same way — [how to rig a Senko](/bass-fishing/how-to-rig-a-senko/) covers the wacky and Texas-style rigs for it. A finesse worm or a drop shot is the option for pressured fish that have seen every lizard in the tackle shop. The presentation is always the same: cast past the bed, bring the bait into it slowly, let it sit, and watch the fish. If the male noses the bait and turns away, lift it and drop it again — the second or third drop is often the one that gets eaten.

## Bed-fishing ethics and the rules

Bed fishing is legal in many states and restricted in others, and the rules are worth checking before you fish the spawn — not after. Some states close bass fishing entirely during the spawn, some allow catch-and-release only, and some allow harvest with restrictions. Wisconsin, for example, sets specific season dates for bass that vary by zone, and its northern zone opens later than its southern zone. Your state's fishing regulations — the booklet or the agency website — are the authority, and they change from year to year, so check the current rules for the water you are fishing rather than relying on what was true last spring.

Beyond the rules, there is the handling. A bass taken off a bed is a bass that is not guarding its eggs, and every minute it spends away from the nest is a minute that bluegill and other predators have free access to it. If you fish beds, handle the fish quickly, keep it in the water as much as possible, and release it right where you caught it — not at the other end of the flat, and not after a photo session on the deck. The male will usually return to the nest if it is released nearby, and the eggs have a far better chance when the guard is back on duty within a minute or two. Some anglers choose not to fish beds at all, and that is a reasonable choice too; the pre-spawn and post-spawn phases offer excellent fishing without ever targeting a nesting fish.

## Post-spawn: the recovery feed

Once the water climbs past the mid-60s and the spawn winds down, the fish slide out to the deep edges off the beds and the nearest cover, in 8 to 15 feet of water, and the post-spawn recovery begins. The females, which lost weight during the spawn, feed hard, and the males stay shallow a little longer to guard the fry. The lures change with the phase: topwater baits at dawn and dusk, when the shad are spawning and the bass are feeding on the surface; swimbaits and swim jigs along the weed edge and the first drop; and the same weightless stick baits and wacky rigs that worked on the beds, now fished around the cover just outside the flats. The full post-spawn picture — the three phases, the fry guarders, and the shad spawn — is covered in [spring bass after the spawn](/bass-fishing/spring-bass-after-the-spawn/).

The post-spawn is also when the shad spawn becomes the best pattern on the lake. Shad spawn by rubbing against hard cover — docks, seawalls, grass lines — in the first hour after sunrise, and the commotion draws bass in numbers. A spinnerbait or a swim jig fished through a shad spawn is one of the highest-percentage presentations of the spring, and it is worth getting on the water early for. ## Cold fronts: the setback every spring angler meets

No spring goes without a cold front, and every cold front costs you fish for a day or two. The pattern is consistent: a front moves through, the air temperature drops, the wind shifts to the north, and the water temperature falls a few degrees. The bass respond by sliding off the flats and back out to the first drop, and the bite slows sharply. The fish do not leave the area — they pull back to the nearest deep water and wait — but they stop feeding aggressively, and the fast retrieves that worked yesterday get ignored today.

The adjustment is to fish deeper and slower. Move out to the staging points and the first drop-off, where the fish have retreated, and slow the presentation down: a jig crawled along the bottom, a suspending jerkbait with pauses of 10 seconds or more, a Texas-rigged worm dragged rather than hopped. FishUSA and other spring guides agree that the fish are still catchable after a front, just deeper and more reluctant, and the anglers who keep catching are the ones who follow the fish out instead of pounding the empty flats. Give the water a day or two to stabilize and warm again, and the fish will push back shallow — usually on the second or third day of the warming trend that follows the front.

The other cold-front lesson is about expectations. A front does not ruin the spring; it pauses it. The fish that were staging before the front are still staging after it, and the spawn that was about to start will start a few days later than it would have. The season is long, and the temperature table still applies — it just runs a few days behind schedule. Fish the water you have, not the water you wish you had, and the spring will come back to you.

## Lure rotation by phase

The lure rotation is the practical summary of this guide: the same fish, in the same lake, in the same month, wants a different lure depending on which phase the water temperature puts it in. The table below is the rotation to carry in your head, or to tape inside the tackle box lid.

| Phase | Water | Lures |
|---|---|---|
| Early pre-spawn | 45–52°F | Suspending jerkbait, jig, lipless crankbait |
| Late pre-spawn | 52–60°F | Shallow crankbait, spinnerbait, bladed jig, jig |
| Spawn | 60–65°F | Lizard, creature bait, wacky rig, finesse worm |
| Post-spawn | 65°F and up | Topwater, swimbait, swim jig, weightless stick bait |

Two things about the rotation are worth noting. First, the jig appears in three phases, because a jig is the most versatile lure in bass fishing — it fishes deep in the cold, it fishes cover in the pre-spawn, and it fishes the grass edge in the post-spawn. If you could carry only one lure all spring, a 3/8 oz jig with a craw trailer would be a reasonable choice. Second, the rotation is a starting point, not a rule: water clarity, wind, and the lake's forage all shift the choices, and the [best bass lures](/bass-fishing/best-bass-lures/) guide ranks the core options by situation for the days when the rotation alone is not enough.

## A spring morning, start to finish

Put the whole guide together and a spring morning has a shape. Check the water temperature before you launch, and let it pick the phase. If the water is in the low 50s, start on the staging points with a jerkbait and a jig, working from deep to shallow. If it is near 60°F, check the flats with polarized glasses for beds, and fish a lizard or a wacky rig slowly through the nests. If it is in the high 60s, start at dawn on the shad spawn with a spinnerbait or a topwater bait, then slide out to the first drop as the sun climbs. If a cold front came through yesterday, start deep and slow, and expect the fish to push shallow again in a day or two.

Watch the signs as you fish. Shad flickering on the surface at dawn means the recovery feed is on. A clean, pale circle on the bottom means a bed, and a fish sitting on it means a male worth a careful cast. A point that drops from 4 feet to 10 feet in a short distance is a staging spot worth fishing thoroughly. The lake tells you where the fish are, one sign at a time, and the temperature tells you which sign to look for. Spring bass fishing is not complicated — it is just temperature, structure, and patience, in that order.
