Every spring lake tells the same story in the same order: bass move shallow to spawn, then slide back out to the first break. The anglers who catch them in the weeks after the spawn are the ones who read that move instead of fishing last week's spots. Post-spawn bass are tired, hungry, and still close to where they spawned — usually on the first drop, point, or weed edge outside the spawning flats, in 4 to 10 feet of water, with the water temperature in the high 60s to low 70s Fahrenheit. Fish slowly, with a weightless stick bait, a small swim jig, or a topwater bait at dawn and dusk, and keep the pauses long.

The post-spawn is one of the most misunderstood windows in bass fishing. Some anglers assume the fish have vanished into deep water; others keep pounding the spawning flats and wonder why the beds are empty. Neither is right. The fish are in between — recovering, feeding, and guarding fry — and they stay catchable for weeks if you understand which phase they are in. This guide breaks the post-spawn into its three phases, explains where the fish go and why, and matches each phase to the presentations that work. For the full spring picture, from the first pre-spawn move to the beds themselves, see [spring bass fishing tips](/bass-fishing/spring-bass-fishing-tips/).

## The three post-spawn phases

Wired2Fish breaks the post-spawn into three smaller phases, and that split is the most useful way to think about the month after the spawn. The phases overlap — on any given lake in late spring, some fish are still guarding fry while others are already feeding hard — but each phase has its own location and its own best presentation, and knowing which one you are fishing decides what you should throw.

| Phase | Timing | What the bass are doing |
|---|---|---|
| Guarding fry | First few days after the spawn | Males stay shallow, guarding balls of fry near cover |
| Recovery feeding | One to three weeks after | Females feed heavily on shad and bluegill near the flats |
| Summer move | Water in the mid-70s and up | Fish slide deeper toward ledges, brush, and summer structure |

The immediate post-spawn is the fry-guarding phase. Once the eggs hatch, the tiny bass school up into tight balls of fry, and the male stays with them, corralling them near cover and chasing off anything that comes close — bluegill, other bass, and even the female that just laid the eggs. Wired2Fish notes that females often turn on their own young and eat them, so the male has real work to do, and he does it for up to a week after the fry leave the nest. These guarding males are aggressive by nature: anything that threatens the fry gets attacked, which makes them catchable on finesse baits fished right through the fry ball. For the lures themselves, the [best bass lures for murky water](/bass-fishing/best-bass-lures-for-murky-water/) guide covers the vibration-first picks that work when the water is still stained from spring rain.

A couple of weeks in, the picture changes. The fry disperse, the males leave them, and the whole population shifts into recovery feeding. The females, which lost weight during the spawn, feed hard — and this is when the shad spawn and the bluegill spawn matter most, because both give post-spawn bass an easy, protein-rich meal in shallow water. Wired2Fish describes this as the phase where bass gorge on shad spawns, bream beds, and other baitfish spawning activity, and it is often the best fishing of the post-spawn window.

The full post-spawn arrives as the water climbs into the mid-70s. The fish start their summer move, sliding deeper toward ledges, brush piles, and the first real summer structure. They do not go far at first — In-Fisherman quotes six-time Bassmaster Classic qualifier Chad Morgenthaler saying post-spawn females initially do not move far from where they spawned, and that he focuses on the 6 to 10 foot range around the nearest drops and flats — but the direction is set: out and down, following the baitfish. In large reservoirs, by midsummer they can be a mile or more from the spawning flats, but in the post-spawn window itself, the fish are still within a short cast of the beds.

## Water temperature: the trigger, not the calendar

Water temperature matters more than the date. Wisconsin's Department of Natural Resources records largemouth bass choosing nest sites when the water reaches 60°F, with eggs laid at 62 to 65°F, and spawning in the state running from late April to early July. The post-spawn begins when that window closes: once the water climbs past the mid-60s and into the low 70s, most largemouth have finished. In the high 60s to low 70s, look for bass suspending near cover in 4 to 8 feet of water, and expect to find fry-guarders in the shallows at the same time as feeding females just outside them.

Measure the temperature where you fish, not at the surface on a sunny afternoon. A surface reading in a shallow bay can run several degrees warmer than the water three feet down, and the fish live in the cooler layer. A simple floating thermometer or the temperature reading on a fish finder gives you the number that matters. Track the trend as well as the reading: a stretch of three or four warm days pushes bass shallow, while a cold front shoves them back out to the first drop. The fish follow the trend, not the forecast, and the best post-spawn days are the second and third days of a warming stretch, when the water has had time to stabilize.

## Start where the spawn happened

Post-spawn bass rarely travel far, so the search starts on the flats that held beds two weeks ago and works outward. Fish the first drop, point, or weed edge outside the spawning flats — the same flats that were full of beds, now empty. A point that juts into the flat, a weed edge that drops from 3 feet to 8 feet, a dock sitting just off the flat: these are the first places a post-spawn bass stops, because they offer depth, cover, and a short trip back to the shallows when the shad start spawning at dawn.

Docks deserve special attention. A dock just outside a spawning flat gives a post-spawn bass shade, vertical cover, and ambush points in one package, and the same dock often holds fish all through the post-spawn window. Skip a weightless stick bait or a wacky rig under the dock and let it fall along the pilings. Wood cover works the same way: laydowns and standing timber near the flats hold recovering females that want cover overhead and deep water a fin-kick away. Grass edges are the third option, especially the outside edge of a weed bed where the grass meets open water — a small swim jig run along that edge is one of the highest-percentage post-spawn presentations there is.

The mistake is fishing the flat itself the way you did during the spawn. The beds are empty, the females have left, and the only fish still on the flat are guarding males — and they are guarding fry, not sitting on beds. Fish the edges and the first break, and save the flat for the first and last hour of light, when the shad spawn pulls bass back up shallow.

## Presentations for tired, hungry fish

Post-spawn bass are tired, and that changes how they want a lure presented. A slow presentation wins here: fish are recovering from the spawn, guarding fry, and not chasing fast lures across open water. The retrieve should be slow, with long pauses, and the lure should stay in the strike zone as long as possible. Three presentations cover most post-spawn situations, and each one matches a phase.

The weightless stick bait is the first choice around fry guarders and in shallow cover. Rig it wacky-style or Texas-style with no weight — [how to rig a Senko](/bass-fishing/how-to-rig-a-senko/) covers both — cast it past a ball of fry, and work it slowly toward them. Wired2Fish recommends exactly this approach: a wacky rig Senko worked past the fry ball is often all it takes to get a guarding male to eat, because the bait reads as a threat to the fry. Around docks and laydowns, the same bait skipped under cover and left to sit is just as effective on recovering females. The [best bass lures](/bass-fishing/best-bass-lures/) guide ranks the weightless stick bait among the top three post-spawn presentations for exactly this reason.

The small swim jig is the tool for the weed edge and the point. A 1/4 oz swim jig with a small swimbait trailer, run slowly along the outside grass line, stays in the strike zone and looks like the shad and bluegill the bass are already eating. Keep the retrieve steady and slow, with the rod tip down, and let the jig tick the tops of the grass. When the jig pulls free of a grass clump, pause — that moment of freedom is when a following fish commits.

Topwater is the third presentation, and it belongs to the first and last hour of light. A walking bait or a popper worked over the flats at dawn, when the shad are flickering on the surface, draws strikes from fish that would ignore the same lure at noon. The same window at dusk works, though dawn is usually better because the shad spawn peaks in the first hour after sunrise and fades as the sun climbs. A buzzbait covers water faster when you are searching for active fish, and a frog over matted grass is the right call when the post-spawn coincides with the first heavy grass growth of the year.

## Retrieve speed and the long pause

If there is one skill that defines post-spawn fishing, it is patience on the retrieve. A weightless stick bait should sit motionless for 10 to 20 seconds between movements. A jig should be crawled, not hopped. A topwater bait should walk slowly, with pauses long enough that you start to wonder whether anything is going to happen — and then one more second. Post-spawn fish are not chasing; they are reacting to something that stays in front of them long enough to be worth eating, or to something that threatens the fry they are guarding.

The pause is also where most bites happen. With a Texas rig or a wacky rig, the bite often comes while the bait is sitting still, and the only sign is the line going slack or twitching sideways. Watch the line, not the rod tip, and when the line moves in a way it should not, reel down until it is tight and set the hook. The full hookset sequence is covered in [how to tie a Texas rig](/bass-fishing/texas-rig-in-a-minute/), and the same reel-down-then-set rule applies to every slow post-spawn presentation.

Speed up only when the fish tell you to. If you are getting follows but no commits, slow down. If you are getting aggressive blowups on topwater, you can fish a little faster. If a cold front has just passed and the fish have slid out to the first drop, slow down again and fish deeper. The fish set the pace; the angler's job is to notice it.

## The shad spawn: the bonus pattern

The shad spawn is the best bonus pattern of the post-spawn, and it is worth planning a morning around. Shad spawn by rubbing against hard cover — a floating dock, a grass line, a seawall, a riprap bank — to work the eggs out of their bellies, and they do it in large groups, usually in the first hour or two after sunrise. The commotion draws bass the way a dinner bell draws a crowd: Wired2Fish describes bass blowing up on preoccupied shad, with one strike drawing in another, until dozens of bass are feeding in one spot. The sign is shad flickering and dimpling on the surface near cover, often with bass visibly busting among them.

When you find a shad spawn, a spinnerbait, a swim jig, or a topwater bait fished right through the commotion is the play. A white or chartreuse spinnerbait matches the shad, and a squarebill crankbait works when the shad are spawning a little deeper on hard bottom. The window is short — most shad spawns fade once the sun gets up — so get on the water early and be ready to move fast when you see the surface activity. If you are reeling in a spinnerbait and shad are crashing into the blades, you are in the right place at the right time.

Bluegill beds are the second bonus pattern. Bluegill spawn in shallow water shortly after the bass, and their beds — clusters of small, light-colored circles on the bottom — draw bass that pick off the spawning bluegill. A small swim jig, a Texas-rigged creature bait, or a topwater frog fished over a bluegill bed can be remarkably productive, and the pattern lasts longer than the shad spawn because bluegill spawn in waves over several weeks. ## Reading the lake: a morning plan

Put it together and a post-spawn morning has a shape. Start at dawn on the flats, fishing topwater and a spinnerbait where the shad are spawning. As the sun climbs and the shad spawn fades, slide out to the first drop and the weed edge, and slow down with a weightless stick bait and a swim jig. Check every dock and laydown just outside the flat. If you see a ball of fry — a dark, shimmering cloud just under the surface — fish a wacky rig through it, because the guarding male is right underneath. By mid-morning, work the points and the deeper grass edges in 6 to 10 feet, where the recovering females are holding.

Watch for the signs that tell you where you are in the phases. Fry balls mean guarding males are still shallow. Shad flickering at dawn means the recovery feed is on. Bass suspending over the first drop in 8 feet of water means the summer move has started. Each sign points to a different presentation, and the angler who reads them spends the morning fishing the right water instead of guessing.
