The other five baits in this guide cover the jobs the squarebill cannot: searching open flats, fishing deeper edges, and calling fish in from a distance with a loud rattle. ## The 6 Best Crankbaits for Muddy Water in Spring

### 1. Strike King KVD 1.5 Squarebill

The KVD 1.5 is the reference squarebill, the bait every other shallow crankbait gets measured against, and it is the first one to tie on in muddy spring water. Its flat-sided body and square lip give it a wide, hunting wobble, and the lip is built to hit wood and rock broadside, stall for a split second, and kick off to the side. That deflection is the whole point in muddy water: a bass that cannot see the bait from more than a foot away feels the bump through its lateral line, turns toward the disturbance, and finds a red crawfish-colored bait sitting right in front of it. The squarebill also fishes where prespawn bass live in spring — laydowns, stump flats, dock posts, and riprap in 2 to 6 feet of water — without hanging up the way a round-lipped diver would.

**Specs:** Tackle Warehouse lists the KVD 1.5 at 2-1/4 inches long, 3/8 oz, diving 3–6 feet, with prices running $9–$11 depending on the version. The standard KVD 1.5 HC is a silent bait with no internal rattles, which is part of why it works on pressured fish, and the KVD 1.5 Hard Knock adds a single loud knocking rattle for the dirtiest water. The color to start with is a red craw pattern — Delta Red or Chili Craw — and Black Back Chartreuse is the backup: Kevin VanDam won the 2011 Bassmaster Classic on the Louisiana Delta cranking a black-back chartreuse KVD 1.5, a result that made the color famous.

**When to throw it:** Around any shallow hard cover in the prespawn and spawn windows. Cast past a laydown or stump, crank the bait into it, and let the lip do the work — the strike usually comes the instant the bait kicks off the wood. Fish it along riprap banks, over the tops of flooded bushes, and beside dock posts. In the dirtiest water, switch to the Hard Knock version so the bait calls fish in from further away.

**Watch out for:** A squarebill is a shallow bait, and it will not reach bass holding deeper than about 6 feet. If the fish are on a channel swing or a deeper point, switch to the Rapala DT6 later in this list. The KVD 1.5 also fishes best on a steady retrieve with the rod tip down; holding the tip high keeps it above its rated depth and out of the strike zone.

### 2. Bill Lewis Rat-L-Trap (1/2 oz)

When the bass are scattered across a flat and you need to find them, the Rat-L-Trap is the search tool. It is a sinking lipless crankbait, so it has no lip to set its depth — you control how deep it runs by counting it down before you start the retrieve. That makes it the most versatile bait on this list: burn it just under the surface over a flooded flat, yo-yo it off the bottom on a point, or rip it through grass and let it flutter down on a slack line. Its tight, high-frequency wiggle and the Liv-N-Sound rattle system produce a baitfish distress sound that bass track through muddy water, and Outdoor Life once named the Rat-L-Trap the most influential fishing lure of all time.

**Specs:** The original 1/2 oz model measures about 3 inches long, and MidwayUSA lists it at $6–$8, making it the cheapest bait in this guide. Tackle Warehouse lists the same model in a $6–$11 range across its color lineup, with the standard colors at the low end. The 1/2 oz size is the all-around pick; the 3/4 oz Mag-Trap suits deeper water and longer casts. Colors for muddy spring water are Red Crawfish and Chrome Blue Back — the red for crawfish season, the chrome for flashing through the stain.

**When to throw it:** Open flats, points, and the mouths of creeks where prespawn bass stage before moving shallow. In spring, burn it across a flat in the afternoon when the sun has warmed the top few feet of water, and slow-roll it along a channel edge on cold mornings. The lipless design also fishes grass well: rip it free of a weed clump and pause, because the strike often comes as the bait flutters down.

**Watch out for:** Two treble hooks grab everything. In thick wood, the Rat-L-Trap snags far more than a squarebill, so keep it over open water or above the grass tops. It also sinks, which means a careless cast into shallow cover ends with the bait on the bottom and the hooks in a stump — count it down deliberately instead of letting it fall on a slack line near cover.

### 3. Strike King Red Eye Shad (1/2 oz)

The Red Eye Shad is the lipless crankbait for anglers who want the rattle and vibration of a Rat-L-Trap with a shad-shaped body and a wider color range. Its free-floating rattles and 3D red eyes give it a loud, distinctive sound, and TackleTour's review of the bait gave it both its Editor's Choice and Best Value awards, praising the price and the way the 1/2 oz size swims straight out of the package. It comes in 1/4, 1/2, and 3/4 oz sizes, and the 1/2 oz is the one most anglers throw because it matches the average size of the baitfish, crawfish, and panfish bass eat in spring.

**Specs:** Sportsman's Warehouse lists the Red Eye Shad in 1/2 oz at 2-1/2 inches long, and Tackle Warehouse lists it at $9–$10 in most colors, with some colors discounted to $7–$10 across retailers. Tackle Warehouse lists it in 60 colors, including Fire Craw, Delta Red, Chart Spring Craw, and Chrome Sexy Shad. The Tungsten 2-Tap version, which replaces the free-floating rattles with a tungsten chamber that produces a double-tapping knock, lists at $10–$11 and is worth the extra money in the muddiest water.

**When to throw it:** Grass edges and flats where bass are feeding on shad or crawfish. Fish it with a yo-yo retrieve: lift the rod to rip the bait up, then let it fall on a semi-slack line and watch for the line to jump. In spring, the Red Eye Shad shines on the flats behind spawning areas, where post-spawn bass scatter and feed — our [spring bass after the spawn](/bass-fishing/spring-bass-after-the-spawn/) guide covers where those fish go.

**Watch out for:** The same treble-hook problem as the Rat-L-Trap, and the Red Eye Shad is slightly more expensive, so losing one to a stump stings a little more. Fish it with the rod tip up when you need to keep it above grass, and clean the hooks after every cast that comes back with salad.

### 4. Rapala DT6

The DT6 is the precision tool in this lineup. Every bait in Rapala's DT (Dives-To) series is built from balsa wood with internal weights placed so the bait dives nose-down to a preset depth and stays there longer than other crankbaits, and the DT6 is preset to 6 feet. That matters in muddy spring water because prespawn bass often stage on a depth break — a creek channel edge, a point that drops from 4 feet to 8 — and a bait that runs at a known depth lets you grind it along that break instead of guessing. Wired2Fish's DT series review notes that the DT6 runs true out of the box and never needs tuning, which is exactly what you want when the water is too dirty to watch the bait swim.

**Specs:** Tackle Warehouse lists the DT6 at 2 inches long, 3/8 oz, diving to 6 feet, with two No. 5 hooks and an internal baritone rattle. MidwayUSA lists it at $9–$11 in colors including Red Crawdad and Silver. The balsa body gives it a livelier action than plastic cranks, and the thin polycarbonate lip gets it down to depth fast, which keeps it in the strike zone longer on every cast.

**When to throw it:** Channel edges, points, and the outside bends of creeks where the bottom drops into the 5- to 8-foot range. Cast across the break, crank the bait down until it ticks the bottom, and keep it grinding along the edge. The DT6 also works over submerged brush piles and rock piles that sit just deeper than squarebill range. In Red Crawdad it doubles as a crawfish imitation for the same spring bite the squarebills cover, just a few feet deeper.

**Watch out for:** Balsa is softer than plastic, so the DT6 chips more easily on rock — check the lip and the paint after a hard day on riprap. It is also a floater at rest, so on the pause it rises instead of suspending; use that rise as part of the retrieve rather than fighting it.

### 5. Lucky Craft LC 1.5 Silent Squarebill

Not every muddy-water situation wants noise. On lakes that get hammered every weekend, or in the pockets of a creek arm where the water runs a little clearer than the main lake, a silent squarebill draws strikes that a rattling bait will not. The Lucky Craft LC 1.5 Silent is the quiet option: the same squarebill shape and deflection as the KVD 1.5, in a silent build for pressured fish. TackleTour's review of the bait measured it at 2.4 inches long and 11.2 grams — about 3/8 oz on their scale, against a rated 1/2 oz — and found it diving 3–4 feet, with a listed price in the $8–$9 range. Wired2Fish's review of the LC Silent Squarebill notes that it deflects easily off hard cover, that its high-float plastic backs it out of snags, and that Lucky Craft has cut the price well below the premium levels the brand once charged.

**Specs:** Tackle Warehouse lists the LC 1.5 Silent at $9–$11; TackleTour's review counts 81 colors across the full range, including red craw patterns such as TO Craw and Delta Crazy Red Craw. The bait carries #4 VMC hooks and a durable plastic squarebill lip. The rattling LC 1.5 DRS version lists at $11–$12 if you want the same body with sound.

**When to throw it:** Clearer pockets inside muddy water, pressured community holes, and anywhere bass have already seen a rattling squarebill that day. Fish it exactly like the KVD 1.5 — into wood and rock, with pauses after each deflection — and let the lack of rattles be the difference. It is also the pick for the coldest spring mornings, when bass are least willing to chase a loud bait.

**Watch out for:** Silent baits give up range in the dirtiest water. If visibility drops below about 6 inches, the rattle on the KVD 1.5 Hard Knock or the Rat-L-Trap will find more fish than a silent bait. Carry the LC 1.5 as a second squarebill, not the only one.

### 6. Megabass S-Crank 1.5

The S-Crank 1.5 is the premium pick, and it earns its place on action alone. Megabass built it around a patented head shape that produces a wide, S-shaped hunting wobble — the bait wanders laterally off course on the retrieve and then hunts back to center, which reads to a bass like a baitfish that has lost its school. Wired2Fish's S-Crank review puts the 1.5 at a 5-foot diving depth, while Megabass USA rates the 1.5 at 4–6 feet, and TackleTour's review gave the S-Crank family an 8.09 out of 10 overall, noting that the 1.2 and 1.5 sizes both come in under $20. The bait runs silent, floats high, and backs out of cover easily, which makes it forgiving around the shallow rock and wood of spring.

**Specs:** Tackle Warehouse lists the S-Crank 1.5 at $19–$20. Megabass USA lists the 1.5 diving 4–6 feet with colors including Fire Craw, PM Gill, Phantom Green Craw, and Mat Shad. Tackle Warehouse and TackleTour both list the 1.5 at 2-1/2 inches and 1/2 oz. The bait takes a split ring on the nose line tie for an even wider, more erratic action — a trick Wired2Fish's review highlights.

**When to throw it:** Riprap banks, chunk rock, and rocky points where the hunting action and the deflection combine. The S-Crank is the bait to tie on when the bass are following squarebills without committing: the wandering action gives them a different look, and the pause after a deflection often seals the deal. It is also a strong pick for smallmouth in rocky spring water, where the same shallow-rock pattern applies.

**Watch out for:** At $19–$20 it costs roughly twice the KVD 1.5, so it is not the bait to throw into a laydown full of unseen snags on the first cast. Learn the cover with the cheaper squarebill first, then send the S-Crank through the spots that produced bumps.

## Why Spring Muddy Water Favors Crankbaits

Spring is the season when crankbaits earn their keep, and muddy water makes the case even stronger. Prespawn bass move shallow in spring — into creeks, bays, and flats in 2 to 8 feet of water — and a crankbait is built to fish exactly that zone, fast enough to cover water and deep enough to stay in front of fish. Our [spring bass fishing tips](/bass-fishing/spring-bass-fishing-tips/) guide covers the seasonal pattern in detail: the fish stage on channel edges, slide onto flats to feed, and hold on the first good cover they find.

Muddy water adds a second reason. When visibility drops to inches, bass stop hunting by sight and start hunting by vibration. A crankbait's wobble, its lip thumping against wood and rock, and its rattle all send pressure waves through the water that a bass feels through its lateral line from several feet away. A soft plastic fished slowly in the same water is nearly invisible; a crankbait announces itself. That is why the rattle matters more in muddy water than in clear water, and why the lipless baits on this list — which are essentially rattles with hooks — cover open flats so well.

The third reason is the crawfish. As the water warms each spring, crawfish become more active, and bass key on them in shallow water. BassResource's lure-color guide explains that crawfish turn reddish as they molt in spring, becoming more vulnerable and more visible, which is why red baits are a must in the spring months. A red crankbait cranked through shallow cover is a crawfish imitation in the exact place and season where bass expect to find crawfish — and in muddy water, the red is also one of the few colors bass can track at all.

## Crankbait Color Rules for Muddy Water

Color selection in muddy water follows one rule: contrast beats realism. BassResource's guide to lure colors by water clarity puts it plainly — solid colors are the way to go in dirty water, and black or black-and-blue are proven colors that show up well in stained and muddy water. Natural shad patterns that look perfect in clear water wash out to gray in the murk, so save them for the clearer pockets and reach for the bright stuff when the water turns brown.

Three colors cover almost every muddy spring situation, and veteran pro Gerald Swindle's rule is that those three — red craw, shad, and firetiger — are the only crankbait colors an angler really needs. Red craw is the early-spring color: Swindle throws a shade of red craw whenever the water temperature sits between 42 and 56 degrees, because that is when crawfish are molting and bass are keyed on them. Firetiger is the dirty-water wildcard: Swindle reaches for it any time the water is heavily stained or looks like chocolate milk, in any month of the year. The chartreuse sides, dark green back, and orange belly give a bass three high-contrast signals in one bait, and the dark back flashing against the bright sides as the bait rocks is visible further than any single color.

Use the water itself as the guide. In lightly stained water with a foot or two of visibility, a red craw or a chartreuse-and-black bait is the pick. In chocolate-milk water with only inches of visibility, firetiger or a solid black bait with a bright accent is the better call — black gives the strongest silhouette against a bright, muddy sky, and the bass finds it by outline as much as by color. When the water temperature climbs past about 58 or 59 degrees, Swindle notes that bass get off the red craw pattern and dial in on shad colors, so switch to a chrome or white bait as the spring warms up and the water clears. ## How to Retrieve a Crankbait in Muddy Spring Water

The retrieve matters as much as the bait, and in cold muddy water the rule is slow to medium. In water under about 55 degrees, bass are sluggish, and a fast retrieve outruns them — the bait passes before the fish can turn and eat it. Crank just fast enough to feel the bait wobbling and ticking cover, and let the lure's own action do the work. A good test is the rod tip: if you can feel the bait thumping steadily, the speed is right; if the rod goes slack and quiet, you are reeling too slowly for the bait to reach its depth.

Deflection is the trigger. Cast past a stump, a rock, or a dock post, crank the bait into it, and let the square lip kick the bait sideways. The sudden change of direction is what makes a bass commit — it reads as a baitfish that just bumped into something and is momentarily vulnerable. After each deflection, pause for a beat. A floating squarebill backs up and rises on the pause, and a following bass often eats it right there, so watch the line for a tick or a sideways jump instead of waiting to feel the bite.

Lipless baits get a different retrieve. The standard approach is the yo-yo: lift the rod tip to rip the bait upward, then drop the tip and let the bait fall on a semi-slack line. The strike usually comes on the fall, so keep a finger on the line and set the hook on anything that feels different. Over grass, the rip matters more than the fall — crank until the bait fouls in a weed clump, rip it free with a sharp pull, and pause, because the flash and the vibration of the bait tearing free is what triggers the strike. Vary the speed until the fish tell you what they want: start slow, speed up a little on each cast, and settle on the pace that draws the first bite.

## Rod, Reel, and Line for Muddy-Water Cranking

The rod for muddy-water cranking is a 7-foot medium-power casting rod with a moderate action. The moderate action — a blank that bends through the middle instead of just at the tip — does two jobs: it lets the bass take the crankbait deep before the hooks set, and it absorbs the headshakes that pull treble hooks free. A fast-action rod works for single-hook baits, but for crankbaits it costs fish. Megabass recommends a 7-foot to 7-foot-4 medium, moderate-action rod for the S-Crank, and that size covers the whole list on this page.

The reel should be a low- to mid-speed baitcaster, around a 5.4:1 to 6.4:1 gear ratio. A slower reel keeps the retrieve in the slow-to-medium range that cold muddy water demands without forcing the angler to think about it, and it also takes the strain off the wrist on a long day of cranking. A high-speed reel has its place for burning a lipless bait across a flat, but for squarebills and divers in spring, the slower reel is the right default.

Line choice changes how deep the bait runs, so pick it deliberately. Fluorocarbon sinks, which helps a crankbait reach its rated depth, and 10- to 12-lb fluorocarbon is the standard for the baits on this list — our [best fluorocarbon line for bass fishing](/bass-fishing/best-fluorocarbon-line-for-bass-fishing/) guide covers the options. Megabass suggests 12- to 16-lb fluorocarbon for the S-Crank, and 15- to 20-lb monofilament when you want to keep the bait running higher in the water column, since mono floats. Thinner line runs a crankbait deeper and thicker line runs it shallower, so if a bait is not reaching the cover, drop a line size before switching baits.

## Quick Picks by Condition

If the water is muddy and the bass are on shallow wood, start with the KVD 1.5 in Delta Red. If the bass are scattered across a flat and you need to find them, tie on the Rat-L-Trap in Red Crawfish and cover water until you get a bite, then slow down and work the area with the squarebill. If the fish are staging a little deeper on a channel edge, the DT6 in Red Crawdad puts a balsa bait right on the break. If the lake gets heavy pressure, the silent LC 1.5 is the change-up. And if the bass are following and not committing, the S-Crank's hunting action is the last card to play. Two baits — a squarebill and a lipless bait, both in red craw — cover most muddy spring days, and the rest of the list fills in the edges.
